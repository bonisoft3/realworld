Conduit — engineering design

Compiled from brief.html · tiers: container · design tokens: DESIGN.md (Press) · team: studio

Overview

Conduit is a public writing community: everyone in it reads everything in it, and everything done about a piece — writing it, revising it, favoriting it, commenting on it, following its author — belongs to one person and is refused to everybody else. The whole app is ten entities, three CDC pipelines, two triggers and twelve screens over the mecha cluster and the omnishell terminal; it targets the container tier only, declares no escape hatch and no handler, and its single deviation from the RealWorld reference is the front door (decision 1): the terminal owns identity, offers exactly passkey and guest, mints the handle itself, and gates every screen or none — so Conduit has no anonymous reader, and a guest click is the whole price of admission. Past that door, Article, Comment, ArticleTag and AppUser are crud tables declared public-read, with their owner-write arms added as additive raw policies rather than a widened SELECT (decision 2); Favorite and Follow are crud and owned, because a private row whose mere visibility answers is this mine is the only thing the interpreter can ask — there is no session placeholder anywhere in the binding grammar. That absence is also why this design carries Me, a live table the brief never names (decision 3): one row per person, RLS-scoped to auth_uid(), provisioned by a trigger inside the auth service's own insert, and read as an empty-or-not probe region whose :empty state is the app's ownership primitive (decision 4). Controls are shown by that probe and refused by RLS anyway (decision 5), which is exactly the brief's "refuses the rest rather than hiding the button". Three live sinks carry what no single user's read can compute: ArticleStats (the favorite count everyone sees, from favorite-recount), TagCount (live articles per tag, from tag-recount), and FavoriteIndex (the cross-user view of favorites that a private Favorite cannot offer, from favorite-index). All three recount absolutely from the source table on every event and upsert on the sink key (decision 11), which is what makes at-least-once delivery indistinguishable from exactly-once; none can chain, because the CDC publication covers crud tables only. Two things are triggers rather than pipelines and each says why: article_tag is delete-and-reinsert inside the article's own transaction because an absolute recount can assert the tags a piece has and can never retract the one it no longer has (decision 10), and me is provisioned synchronously because a guest must not wait seconds for their own settings screen to exist. On the surface, twelve screens (home, feed, editor, settings, older, feed-older, article, edit-article, profile, profile-favorites, tag, search) share one preview fragment, one favorite pill, one follow control and one search box, so a list reads the same everywhere it appears — and that fragment's byline is a link into profile, which is the only door either profile route has, since both are parametrized and therefore out of the nav; the reading modes are two routes rather than two lists on one screen, because two top-level regions would fight over one state machine and the loser would be the feed's empty state — the one the brief singles out (decision 18). Article bodies are markdown: the body carries data-text-format="markdown", backed by a renderer module this app declares on the article screen, and the terminal builds its nodes with createElement/textContent, so there is no sanitizer to design and a body containing markup renders as the characters its writer typed (decision 16). Long lists page by keyset cursor — the link on the twentieth card carries that row's own created_at into /older/:when, so the number a screen cannot compute is a value it already holds; feed pages to its own feed-older rather than to the community list, because a continuation that quietly drops the follow filter is worse than none (decision 20). The identity is Press (decision 26): warm paper, one pine accent spent only on creation, and a serif/sans duality that lets a reader tell authored text from chrome without reading a word — a long scroll of previews is the app's most common surface and the article body is its most important one. What the screens do not build is as deliberate as what they do: the nav row, the back stack, sign-out, the eight-state machine, row motion, the unconfirmed-write badge, the dead-gateway backoff, the dark palette and the storybook are all the terminal's, and reimplementing one would be a review finding rather than independence (decision 24). Escape hatches: none, argued rather than assumed (decision 27).

Auth

auth service · mecha cluster

The identity box, all platform doctrine — the brief designs no login screen and this ir adds none, which is Conduit's one deviation from the RealWorld reference and is argued rather than assumed (decision 1). The mecha auth service sits behind the cluster's Caddy at /auth/*; the terminal owns the door and offers exactly two gestures — one tap on a discoverable passkey, or one click on guest, which mints the same generated identity with no ceremony at all. Nobody types an identifier, a password or an email: the server generates the friendly handle (velvet-crow-4) and regenerates on collision, so no two people share one and a handle can be a byline the moment it exists. Tokens are JWT HS256 with claims {role, sub, handle, exp}; PostgREST derives auth_uid() from sub, and every RLS policy and every default auth_uid() below is written against it — no screen ever carries the reader's id, because nothing in the binding grammar could hold it. The service's insert is (id, handle) and nothing else, which is why every other AppUser column is nullable or defaulted, and the 014 trigger rides that same transaction to provision Me — the reader's own row exists on the first paint after a guest click (decision 3). The shell chrome renders “<display name> · <handle> · sign out” on every screen, and beside it the nav it builds from the non-parametrized routes in declaration order — Community · Your Feed · New Article — which is why the roster is ordered the way it is and why settings draws no sign-out button of its own (decision 24). The reader's own name there is a link, to their own profile: capabilities.auth.self hands the terminal the route (/profile/:handle, its param filled from the session user) and the column the chosen name lives in (app_user.display_name), read live rather than stamped from the token — whose claims are fixed for the session, so a rename would otherwise be invisible in the one place a person is named on every screen. settings is off the strip for the same reason: one's own page already leads there (decision 28). Storyboard frames elide shell chrome by convention: it is shell-owned, identical on every route, and drawn only in the home frames as the representative — one sticky strip at the top of the frame, nav labels from the left and “<display name> · <handle> · sign out” pushed right, with every screen's own content beginning below it; every other screen draws its back-link and nothing else. Sign out clears the stored token, takes the strip and every held screen down with it, and replaces the document, so the door is reached with nothing of the session still standing; any 401 does the same. Nothing lives client-side but the token, so signing back in with the same passkey finds every article, follow and favorite exactly where it was left. Pipelines are nobody: their reads and writes carry SERVICE_JWT (role service, all-zero sub), which passes every policy — that unrestricted read is precisely what lets a pipeline compute the cross-user joins a single reader's screen cannot state (decision 6).

Entities

Entities are declared parents-first — AppUser and Me, then Article, then its children — so DDL emission in declaration order satisfies the foreign keys. Each entity carries data-access, the RLS mode its policies are emitted from; the service role passes all of them. All primary keys are client-minted uuids — the shell mints ids so retries and duplicate deliveries stay idempotent — except the three pipeline sinks, whose key is their grouping key as text; a sink that a screen reads as a list must name that key id, because a list region keys its row nodes on String(row.id) and a sink without one collapses every row onto a single node (decision 12). Four tables are declared public-read, which emits a SELECT arm for app_user and no write arm at all; their owner-write arms arrive as additive raw policies, never as a widened SELECT over an owned table (decision 2).

AppUser entity · crud

One person, by the name Conduit gave them. Minted by the auth service, which inserts (id, handle) and nothing else — so every other column is nullable or defaulted, or sign-in itself fails. It is crud rather than live because settings writes it through PostgREST. Access: public-read — a byline is not a private fact. The handle, display name and picture render on every preview, every article, every comment and the strip that names the reader themselves, and “editing a display name changes how that person reads everywhere they appear” is only free because each of those surfaces reads this same row rather than a copy. The owner's UPDATE arm arrives in 011_owner_writes.sql, gated id = auth_uid(); there is deliberately no INSERT and no DELETE arm — identities are minted under the service role and never removed. An RLS policy decides which row may be updated and never which columns, and 002_grants.sql emits a table-wide GRANT … UPDATE ON ALL TABLES, so the absent form field is not what keeps the handle fixed: 011 also carries the column privileges REVOKE UPDATE ON app_user FROM app_user; GRANT UPDATE (display_name, bio, image_url) ON app_user TO app_user;. That is narrowing, so it is safe under decision 2's own rule, and PostgREST answers 42501 on a PATCH naming any other column — which is what makes test-handle-not-writable an enforced fact rather than a convention.

Me entity · live

The reader's own row — the most load-bearing table in this design, and one the brief never names (decision 3). The terminal publishes no session binding: lookup resolves param.* and dot paths into the current row and nothing else, so a screen literally cannot ask “is this mine?”. Conduit asks it four times — edit and delete on your own piece, the × on your own comment, edit your profile instead of follow on your own page, and a /settings route with no :param to identify you with. me answers all four the only way the platform allows: a row RLS-scoped to auth_uid() whose mere visibility, joined against an author_id or a handle, is the answer, read as a probe region whose :empty state CSS branches on (decision 4). All three filters (id=eq.{author_id}, handle=eq.{param.handle}, and the bare read on settings) are in the locally translatable subset with no select, so they run in-tab against the synced collection — no round trip per card. Access: owned on id; 012_me_readonly.sql then drops the INSERT/UPDATE/DELETE arms owned emits, because narrowing is always safe and nothing should ever write this table from a browser. Its writer is the 014 trigger, inside the auth service's own insert — a trigger and not a pipeline because a guest must not wait a CDC round trip for their own settings screen to exist. live also keeps it out of the CDC publication, which is right: nothing derives from it. The entity declares writers: "pipeline" — the non-forms arm of a two-value vocabulary (schema.cue:79), whose default is "forms". It is the one field a reviewer greps to find the client-writable tables, and leaving it defaulted would have named the one table whose entire design is that no browser writes it; the actual writer is the 014 trigger and 012 is the enforcement.

Article entity · crud

One piece of writing. crud because forms write it and because it must emit CDC — ArticleTag is maintained by a trigger on this table, and that trigger's writes are what feed tag-recount. Access: public-read — the community reads everything; INSERT, UPDATE and DELETE arms arrive in 011 gated author_id = auth_uid(), so a save against somebody else's piece matches no policy, PostgREST answers 4xx, and edit-article says so out loud rather than hiding the control — which is what the brief asks for (decision 5). Deleting a piece takes its comments, favorites and tag rows with it: every ref emits ON DELETE CASCADE, so there is no cleanup to write.

ArticleTag entity · crud

The queryable projection of Article.tags. It exists because three things need tag rows and none of them can have a text column: the tag list's inner embed, the chips on every preview (a nested region cannot split a string — there is no computation in markup), and tag-recount, which needs a crud-path source because the CDC publication covers crud tables only. Access: public-read with no write arm at all — every row is written by the 013 SECURITY DEFINER trigger inside the article's own transaction, and a browser has no business inserting one (decision 10).

One reply under one article. Everyone reads them; only the writer removes one. Bodies are plain text, not markdown (decision 17).

Favorite entity · crud

One person saying a piece is worth it. Access: owned on user_id — private, and that is the whole design (decision 6). The favorite control must know whether this reader has favorited this article on every card of every list, and the only free answer is a nested region whose rows RLS has already narrowed to mine: article_id=eq.{id} is in the translatable subset with no select, so it resolves in-tab against the synced collection — no round trip per card, and the optimistic insert flips the pill on the press rather than after the write lands. The public faces of a favorite are derived instead: the count everyone sees is ArticleStats, and the list of what a person has favorited is FavoriteIndex. That split is the point — the hot per-card “is it mine” read is local and private; the cold cross-user read is a pipeline sink.

UNIQUE (user_id, article_id) — at most one favorite per person per article, a composite unique index in assembly SQL (decision 14). The refusal path is exact and worth stating: the Caddyfile injects Prefer: return=representation,resolution=ignore-duplicates on POSTs, and ignore-duplicates targets the primary key, which a client-minted uuid never collides on — so a duplicate favorite reaches the composite index, raises 23505 and comes back 4xx as a readable refusal rather than a silent merge. With the toggle reading its true state that happens only on a double-press inside the optimistic window, where a refusal is honest.

Bookmark entity · crud

A piece set aside to finish. Access: owned on user_id — and where Favorite is private at the root and public in its two derived faces, a bookmark is private all the way down (decision 35). Nobody may see that you saved something, no count moves when you do, and so there is no sink, no pipeline and no lag: the table the star writes is the table reading-list reads. That is what lets the list be ordered by save time, which is what a reader means by “my list”, where profile-favorites must take its order from a sink that only knows when the favorite happened to reach it.

Structurally it is the favorite pattern with the derived half deleted, and it inherits that pattern's virtues: article_id=eq.{id} with no select is in the translatable subset, so the per-card “have I saved this” probe resolves in-tab against rows RLS has already narrowed to mine — no round trip per card, and the optimistic insert fills the star on the press. However many cards a screen draws, they share one shape, because a shape is per table.

FavoriteCount entity · live

What a reader may know about their own place in a public number: whether the count included them, and the total that count came from. Written only by favorite-count (writers: pipeline), owned by user_id, so each browser syncs one row per piece it has favorited and no more. A count discloses nobody, so this leaks nothing that decision 18 protects.

mine_counted and total_at_read are carried together because they must come from ONE read to be usable as a pair; as_of_txid names the version of the reader's own row the pair speaks for, which is how the terminal tells a pair that still describes its row from one that has been overtaken. Keyed "<user>:<article>" so a repeated read upserts rather than accumulating (decision 20), and carrying no foreign key for the reason decision 21 gives.

ArticleStats entity · live

The favorite count per article — the same number for everybody, which is exactly what a private Favorite cannot produce. Written only by favorite-recount (writers: pipeline); the browser reads it as a nested singleton in each arm of the pill with data-empty-row='{"favorite_count":0}', so a piece nobody has favorited renders 0 rather than nothing — a per-subject sink has no seed, its row appears with the first event. Both arms are siblings of the favorite probe rather than children of it (decision 4), so article_id=eq.{id} resolves against the article row in both — the one binding this sink has, and the one a set arm nested inside the probe would have silently got wrong, rendering 0 on exactly the pieces a reader has favorited.

FavoriteIndex entity · live

The cross-user view of favorites: one row per (person, article) pair that has ever been favorited, carrying whether it still is. It exists because the brief asks for “the ones they have favorited” on anybody's profile while Favorite is private by design — this is the platform's own answer to a query a single reader's screen cannot state, a join materialised by a pipeline reading with the SERVICE_JWT and seeing every row (decision 6). Written only by favorite-index. Read as a list on profile-favorites, each row nesting a one-row list over Article so the preview is always current rather than a stale copy. Accepted loss: the list lags a favorite by pipeline latency, seconds — no acceptance id demands otherwise, and the reader's own pill on the card is optimistic regardless.

The one rule on a favorite that no CHECK can state: the article's author is a row away. Pipelines

All three are cdc-triggered and share one shape: a redis-streams input on cdc-events with its own consumer group, a branch that GETs ${CRUD_URL}/<source>?<aggregate> with Bearer ${SERVICE_JWT} and grafts the result at root.rows, one bloblang mapping, and a retrying POST to ${CRUD_URL}/<sink>?on_conflict=<pk> carrying Prefer: resolution=merge-duplicates — straight to PostgREST, never through Caddy, whose client-facing Prefer injection would clobber the upsert. Three properties hold for all three and are stated once here. The read is absolute — the CDC event is a trigger, never a delta, so the emitted rows are a pure function of the source table's current contents (decision 11). The event's own key is unioned into the emitted set, which is the only way a delete-to-zero can overwrite a stale final value; this depends on the restore-deleted-row processor back-filling the after-image from the before-image on deletes. And data-from is documentation, not a filter: every pipeline's input is the whole cdc-events stream, so without a guard any crud-table event would re-run every aggregate. Reasserting the current truth is not free — six tables are in the publication, resolution=merge-duplicates is ON CONFLICT DO UPDATE, and that fires the emitted txid restamp trigger on every sink row it touches, so one comment insert would give every article_stats, favorite_index and tag_count row a fresh WAL version and Electric would fan the whole sink out to every connected tab. So each mapping's guard drops the message rather than emitting a no-op: root = deleted() when the event row does not come from the pipeline's own source table, which costs one GET and no write. Each pipeline below names the discriminating column it tests, and the maintenance trap is recorded there too — a future crud table carrying that column re-opens the storm. The one standing prohibition: the publication is FOR TABLE <crud tables>, so a sink can never be a source — TagCount cannot derive from ArticleStats, and ArticleTag had to be a crud table precisely so that it could be one.

favorite-count pipeline

On the same Favorite events as favorite-recount, and from its own absolute read, emit one FavoriteCount row for the reader who acted: whether that read counted their favorite, and the total it counted. Only the actor's row is written, so this costs one row per event and never fans out across a piece's other favoriters.

It exists because the difference between a public total and a reader's own contribution is not recoverable from the reader's row: it is a property of the READ that produced the total, not of the data now, and a bounded row cannot carry unbounded history (decision 37). Stating it here is what lets the terminal maintain others — a quantity no write of the reader's can change — and apply their intent on top of it live.

favorite-recount pipeline

On every Favorite CDC event — insert or delete, both matter because pressing the pill again must lower the number — re-read select=article_id over the whole table under the service token (which sees every person's rows, unlike any screen), group by article_id in bloblang, union the event row's own article_id into the emitted set, and emit an array of complete {article_id, favorite_count} sink rows upserted with ?on_conflict=article_id, consumer group realworld-favorite-recount. The mapping's first act is the source discriminator: user_id occurs on no other crud table in this schema (Article and Comment carry author_id, Follow carries follower_id), so root = deleted() unless the event row has one — an Article or Comment event costs one GET and no write. Adding a crud table with a user_id column re-opens the write amplification and is the one change this guard must be re-read for. The union is what makes the transform total over its sink: the last un-favorite drops that article out of the grouping entirely, so without the event's key no row is emitted for it and the count freezes at 1 forever — “pressing again takes it back” fails at exactly the boundary a reviewer would test. The mapping also throws rather than proceed if the harness handed it a bare array at the root, because that means the CDC event was discarded and only the groups survive, which would ship the same stale-final bug silently. Idempotence: every emitted row carries an absolute count keyed by the sink's primary key, and the keyed upsert overwrites by that key — replaying any event, or any unrelated one, recomputes identical rows, so at-least-once delivery is indistinguishable from exactly-once and out-of-order delivery converges because no value depends on arrival order; nothing is ever incremented. A browser shim (pipelines/favorite-recount.browser.js) is required to exist by the emitter and throws here — the browser tier's shim contract is one row keyed on id, which a keyed array cannot satisfy, and this app targets container only (decision 25). Shaped per pipeline-designer.

favorite-index pipeline

The same trigger, a different question: not “how many”, but “which pairs”. On every Favorite event, re-read select=user_id,article_id,created_at over the whole table under the service token, emit one active: true row per current favorite keyed <user_id>:<article_id>, and — when the triggering event's pair is not among them — one row for that pair with active: false. It carries the same user_id discriminator as favorite-recount and for the same reason — an unrelated event is dropped, not upserted — which matters most here, because this sink grows in the number of pairs ever favorited and reasserting it in full on every comment would be the largest write in the app. That second case is how an upsert-only sink retracts: it covers both un-favoriting and an article deletion cascading its favorites away, so the pair a reader last saw on somebody's profile disappears from the list rather than being orphaned there. Every emitted object carries the same five keys, because PostgREST rejects a bulk insert whose objects disagree on their key set — which is why an active: false row repeats the pair's own columns instead of being a partial patch. Upserted ?on_conflict=id, consumer group realworld-favorite-index. Idempotence: absolute over Favorite and keyed by the sink's primary key, exactly as favorite-recount; the only state not recomputed from the source is an active: false row for a pair nothing references any more, and re-favoriting that pair re-emits it as active in the same absolute pass. The sink grows in the number of pairs ever favorited and never in the number of events. Browser shim pipelines/favorite-index.browser.js, throwing, per decision 25.

tag-recount pipeline

On every ArticleTag CDC event, re-read select=article_id,tag over the whole table and emit, for each distinct tag, {id: <tag>, article_count: <distinct article_ids wearing it>}, unioning the triggering event's tag so the last piece to shed a tag drives its count to zero rather than leaving it at one. Upserted ?on_conflict=id, consumer group realworld-tag-recount. Its discriminator is tag, which occurs only on ArticleTag among the crud tables (Article carries the text column tags, not tag): root = deleted() without it, so a favorite or a comment never rewrites the popular-tags panel. Deriving from ArticleTag rather than re-splitting Article.tags in bloblang is deliberate: the 013 trigger is the single definition of “what tags does this piece wear”, and a second splitter would be free to disagree with the tag screen's list. Retagging and deletion both reach this pipeline as ordinary article_tag INSERT/DELETE events — the trigger's delete-and-reinsert, and the foreign-key cascade — which is exactly what keeps the popular-tags panel honest “as articles are published, retagged and deleted”. Idempotence: the count is a set cardinality computed absolutely from the source plus the event's key and upserted by the sink's primary key, so duplicate article_tag rows could not inflate it even if the composite unique index allowed them, and replaying any event recomputes every tag's count from scratch. Browser shim pipelines/tag-recount.browser.js, throwing, per decision 25.

Two structural rules. A region that must contain another region is a one-row list region, never a singleton: the singleton branch of the interpreter binds attributes and never recurses, so a nested region under a singleton is dead markup that lints clean (decision 19); this is also why article reports empty rather than gone for a removed piece. And a probe region's :empty state is the ownership and toggle primitive — a region with rows and no data-empty is CSS-:not(:empty), so both arms of every owner-only control and both toggles sit in the markup and CSS chooses between them, with no session placeholder and no second read. The two probe shapes place their control differently and decision 4 now says so per shape: an ownership probe's control is a plain sibling wired to the enclosing row, and a toggle probe's retraction is a sibling carrying data-filter. Every screen carrying a probe also carries [data-state="loading"] .arms { visibility: hidden }, because until the first refresh sweeps the <template> the region reads non-empty and the unset arm would flash.

The accent is spent once per screen. DESIGN.md gives the accent to creation and commitment and forbids it on navigation, and this ir holds one rule across every frame: a screen shows the accent on the control that commits, plus its own success ring, and nowhere else; every navigation link is secondary with a border hairline underline. So settings's “View your public page →” and editor's latest-piece link are secondary, not accent. The single exception is named here rather than left to be inferred: a screen whose empty state carries no committing control at all may put the accent on its one way out (feed, tag, search, older, article, edit-article), because there the accent is still the screen's only call to action.

Invariants & test pairs

Prose invariants with embedded CEL, translated to executable tests at the program rung (the generated tests back-reference these ids). Bindings are injected, never ambient: input is the given data plus input.now for the clock, output the result, error the failure (error.kind, error.field). Four kinds of failure recur and are spelled the same way throughout: 'check_violation' (a row CHECK or a NOT NULL), 'unique_violation' (a composite unique index), 'rls_denied' (a write matching no policy — PostgREST answers 4xx and the shell paints .store-error alongside the validation-error state), and 'unauthorized' (no token; HTTP 401). error.field is spelled one way per kind, so two sibling invariants cannot describe the same 23505 differently: for 'check_violation' it is the column name, and for 'unique_violation' it is the constraint name (uq_favorite_pair, uq_follow_pair, uq_article_tag_pair) — never a column list, which the program rung would otherwise have to invent a second mapping for. Where a paragraph is about what the reader sees rather than what the store holds, output is the rendered node summary — output.elements (tag names) and output.text — and the paragraph says so. Every read named below is the exact fragment in the owning screen's data-extract="reads" list; no invariant invents a query. There are no handlers in this app, so every test lives in this section.

auth

One tap on the passkey, or one click on guest, completes the ceremony and yields a token whose bearer is a person the community can name: output.handle.size() > 0 && output.role == 'app_user' && output.sub == output.user_id — nothing was typed, no email, no password, no chosen username. The auth service inserts AppUser (id, handle) and the 014 trigger provisions Me in the same transaction, so output.me.id == output.user_id holds on the first paint rather than after a CDC round trip — which is what lets a guest reach settings and their own profile immediately. The handle stands in the chrome beside sign out on every screen until they sign out — under the display name once they set one, and linking to their own profile either way; the terminal draws it, and no screen redraws it.

Signing out clears the stored token and returns to the door; a store request made afterwards is refused rather than answered — error.kind == 'unauthorized', and output == null. Signing back in with the same passkey re-issues the same sub (output.user_id == input.user_id_before), and because every article, follow and favorite is a Postgres row scoped by auth_uid() and nothing is ever client-local, the first loads return the state exactly as it was left: output.articles == input.articles_before && output.follows == input.follows_before && output.favorites == input.favorites_before. The regression this catches is any drift toward browser-held state — a cached follow set, a locally remembered favorite — which would survive a sign-out it has no business surviving.

AppUser

Setting a display name, an about line and a picture writes one row and changes nothing else: output.display_name == input.display_name && output.bio == input.bio && output.image_url == input.image_url && output.handle == input.handle && output.id == input.id. Because every byline, comment and preview embeds author:app_user(handle,display_name,image_url) rather than denormalising a copy, the change reaches all of them at once, with no reload and nothing to invalidate: output.previews.all(p, p.author.display_name == input.display_name) && output.comments.all(c, c.author.display_name == input.display_name). The regression this catches is any future denormalisation of a name onto Article or Comment, which would pass every screen test and quietly strand old rows.

The handle is server-generated and never changes, and the mechanism is a column privilege, not an absent form field and not the row policy. An RLS policy gates which row may be updated and never which columns, so 011's USING (id = auth_uid()) would pass a hand-made PATCH /crud/app_user?id=eq.<me> {"handle":"…"} on the reader's own row — the case this paragraph exists to pin, and the one a row policy cannot cover. 011 therefore carries REVOKE UPDATE ON app_user FROM app_user; GRANT UPDATE (display_name, bio, image_url) ON app_user TO app_user;, and PostgREST honours it: error.kind == 'rls_denied' && output.handle == input.handle for that PATCH, with the row otherwise untouched (output.display_name == input.display_name). A write against somebody else's row is refused by the row policy as well: error.kind == 'rls_denied', and their row is untouched. Both arms matter and fail independently — dropping the column grant leaves the first passing at the database and silently untrue, which is what makes Me.handle's “cannot drift” safe to rely on and every /profile/<handle> link permanent.

Me

Me is the app's only answer to "is this mine?", and it answers it by visibility rather than by a placeholder the interpreter does not have. Read under a person's own token it yields exactly one row — output.size() == 1 && output[0].id == input.user_id — and read under anybody else's it yields none: output == []. So the three probes are exact rather than approximate: me?id=eq.{author_id} is non-empty precisely when the reader wrote the row it sits in (output.size() == (input.author_id == input.user_id ? 1 : 0)), and me?handle=eq.{param.handle} is non-empty precisely on one's own profile. Nothing writes this table from a browser — 012 drops the insert, update and delete arms, so error.kind == 'rls_denied' for every attempt — which is what stops a person deleting the row that decides their own controls.

Article

Publishing from {title: 'On writing', body: '# hi', tags: 'craft'} stamps the clock and the author from the JWT, never from the form: output.created_at == input.now && output.updated_at == input.now && output.author_id == input.user_id && output.slug.size() > 0. The row matches the community read's filter with no further condition, so it is on home for every reader at once: output.community.exists(a, a.id == input.id) under a second person's token, without a reload. The writer's landing is one tap, not zero, because no form can navigate (decision 21). The landing line is a local region — author_id=eq.{id} with no limit and no select, so it resolves against the synced collection — and it flips to the new title inside the optimistic window: output.latest[0].id == input.id && output.latest[0].title == input.title. The anchor is the boundary this paragraph exists to pin. slug is generated and carries no form field, so an unconfirmed row has none, and an anchor offered in that window would resolve to #/article/, which matchRoute rejects and currentRoute() throws on: output.latest[0].pending == (output.latest[0].slug == null) and output.landing.pending == false || output.landing.href == '' — no tappable address is ever offered while the row is unconfirmed, which the screen achieves with [data-pending="true"] .landing { display: none }. Once the txid settles the badge clears and the address is the real one: output.landing.href == '/article/' + output.slug. The gesture-cost difference from an automatic navigation is argued in decision 21 and signed in the qa review; this paragraph states the behaviour rather than claiming the sign-off.

An article with no title or no body is refused and nothing is saved. Both arms fail, separately and for the same reason: error.kind == 'check_violation' && error.field == 'title' for {title: '', body: 'words'}, and error.kind == 'check_violation' && error.field == 'body' for {title: 'On writing', body: ''}; in both cases output == null and the table is unchanged. Whitespace is not content — {title: ' ', body: '\n'} is refused identically, because the shell trims every text and textarea value before submit and the SQL check is written over btrim as well, so the guard is total whichever path the write took. The required constraints are this.size() > 0 && this.size() <= 200 on title and this.size() > 0 on body; description and tags are optional and a piece with neither still publishes (error == null), which is the boundary the native required attributes must not over-reach.

The address is readable and derived from the title, and it is unique without a uniquifying loop no generated column could express. For {title: 'How to Train Your Dragon!'} the stored column is output.slug.matches('^[a-z0-9]+(-[a-z0-9]+)*-[0-9a-f]{8}$') && output.slug.startsWith('how-to-train-your-dragon-') — lower-cased, every run of non-alphanumerics collapsed to one hyphen, the readable half capped at 80 characters, then the first hex group of the row's own uuid. Two articles sharing that title still have addresses that tell them apart: output.a.slug != output.b.slug && output.a.slug.startsWith('how-to-train-your-dragon-') && output.b.slug.startsWith('how-to-train-your-dragon-') (measured: …-4f5df9a9 and …-7f2af670). A title with no alphanumerics at all still yields an address rather than an empty one: {title: '!!!'} gives output.slug.startsWith('article-'), which is the arm the nullif/coalesce pair exists for and the one a naive regexp-only expression would return empty. The DDL UNIQUE is the backstop for the vanishing case where two same-titled pieces draw the same hex prefix: error.kind == 'unique_violation' reaches the writer as a readable refusal rather than a corrupted route.

Saving a retitled piece recomputes the address and keeps the identity: output.id == input.id && output.slug != input.slug && output.created_at == input.created_at && output.updated_at == input.now — the column is GENERATED ALWAYS … STORED, so it moves on UPDATE, and updated_at moves because the revise form submits a hidden {now} (a column default fires only on INSERT). The edit reaches every surface the piece appears on without a reload, because each of them reads the same row rather than a copy: output.community.exists(a, a.id == input.id && a.title == input.title) && output.tag_list.exists(a, a.id == input.id && a.title == input.title) && output.profile.exists(a, a.id == input.id && a.title == input.title). This is also why edit-article is keyed on the id and not the slug: a slug-keyed editor would match zero rows the instant the retitle landed and blank itself (output.size() == 1 for id=eq.{param.id} after the save, output.size() == 0 for the old slug).

Only the author changes or removes a piece, and Conduit refuses the rest out loud rather than hiding the control. Under the author's token the update lands (error == null && output.title == input.title); under anybody else's it matches no policy — error.kind == 'rls_denied' && output == null — and the row is byte-identical afterwards: output.row.title == input.title_before && output.row.body == input.body_before && output.row.updated_at == input.updated_at_before. The same holds for delete. Reaching /editor/:id by hand for somebody else's piece is therefore not an exploit but a refusal the writer can read ("This piece isn't yours to change."), which is what edit-article's validation-error frame storyboards. The screen still offers the right control to the right person via the Me probe: hiding and refusing are done in that order, not as alternatives (decision 5).

Deleting a piece removes the row cleanly — output == null && error == null — and every FK declared on it carries ON DELETE CASCADE, so its comments, favorites and tag rows go with it in the same statement: output.comments == [] && output.favorites == [] && output.article_tags == [] for that article id. Every list the piece appeared in drops it without a reload — community, feed, its tags, its author's profile, search: output.community.all(a, a.id != input.id) && output.feed.all(a, a.id != input.id) && output.tag_list.all(a, a.id != input.id) && output.profile.all(a, a.id != input.id) && output.search.all(a, a.id != input.id). The derived tables follow a beat later by their own pipelines (test-tag-recount-zero, test-favorite-index-retracts), which is the one part of this that is eventually consistent and is stated rather than hidden.

ArticleTag

The words typed on an article file it under each of them at once. From {tags: 'dragons Training,, how-to'} the 013 trigger writes three rows inside the article's own transaction — output.size() == 3 && output.exists(t, t.tag == 'dragons') && output.exists(t, t.tag == 'training') && output.exists(t, t.tag == 'how-to') — lower-cased, split on every run of non-[a-z0-9-], intra-word hyphens kept, empties dropped, and duplicates collapsed (output.all(t, output.filter(u, u.tag == t.tag).size() == 1), which uq_article_tag_pair would refuse anyway). Because the write is in the same transaction as the article, the piece is on each tag's list the moment it is on the community list: output.tag_list('dragons').exists(a, a.id == input.id). The regression this catches is a second definition of "what tags does this piece wear" — a bloblang re-splitting the text column would be free to disagree with the list read (decision 10).

Retagging replaces the set exactly rather than adding to it. Saving {tags: 'dragons how-to'} over {tags: 'dragons training how-to'} leaves output.size() == 2 && output.all(t, t.tag in ['dragons', 'how-to']) — the delete-and-reinsert is what makes the retraction exact, and it is the reason this projection is a trigger and not a pipeline: an absolute recount can assert the pairs a piece has and can never retract the pair it no longer has, because the CDC event carries the after-image only. Saving a title fix without touching the words churns nothing: output.article_tags == input.article_tags_before — the IS NOT DISTINCT FROM guard means the editor naming tags on every save costs no DOM replacement and wakes no pipeline. That guard is load-bearing in the other direction too: the revise form must always submit tags, because the trigger fires on the column being named, and a form that dropped it would silently freeze the projection.

Comment

Leaving a comment lands it under the piece at once and clears the form. The row carries the article from its enclosing region and the writer from the JWT, neither from a typed field: output.article_id == input.article_id && output.author_id == input.user_id && output.created_at == input.now && output.body == input.body, and the comment region's read returns it without a reload: output.comments.exists(c, c.id == input.id). The form is cleared by the terminal on success, not by the screen, so nothing typed is lost on a refusal.

An empty comment is refused and nothing is saved: error.kind == 'check_violation' && error.field == 'body' && output == null for {body: ''} and, identically, for {body: ' '} — the check is written over btrim, so whitespace is not content here either. The native required attribute refuses the first case before it is sent and the database refuses both; the two land on the same validation-error treatment, so there is one refusal to review, not two.

Only a comment's own writer is offered its removal, and removing it takes it away on the spot: under the writer's token output == null && error == null and the region loses the row without a reload (output.comments.all(c, c.id != input.id)); under anybody else's token the delete matches no policy — error.kind == 'rls_denied' — and the comment is still there afterwards: output.comments.exists(c, c.id == input.id). There is no update arm at all, in any policy, so a comment can be removed and rewritten but never edited — the absent policy is what keeps that true even if a form were ever added. The × is a sibling of the Me probe and never its child; a form inside the probe's item template would be wired against the me row and delete the wrong thing, which is the exact regression this paragraph exists to catch.

Comments read oldest first, quantified over every adjacent pair — range(output.size() - 1).all(i, output[i].created_at <= output[i + 1].created_at) — so a reply follows what it replies to. A piece nobody has answered returns the empty collection without erroring (output == [] && error == null) and the region's data-empty sentence says so and invites the first ("No one has said anything yet. Say the first thing."), with the live comment box beneath it — a designed frame, never a bare form and never a blank.

Favorite

Favoriting creates one row for this reader and this piece — output.user_id == input.user_id && output.article_id == input.article_id && output.created_at == input.now — and the control fills on the press rather than after the write lands, because the probe reads the optimistic row from the synced collection (output.probe.size() == 1). Pressing again takes it back: the row-scoped delete leaves output == null && output.probe.size() == 0, and the pill returns to its unset arm. The round trip touches nothing else — the article row is unchanged (output.article == input.article_before) — and the number in the pill comes from ArticleStats a beat later, which is the honest story the frames draw.

A person may favorite an article at most once, however many times they press. The second insert of the same pair raises the composite unique index — error.kind == 'unique_violation' && error.field == 'uq_favorite_pair', the constraint name, spelled exactly as test-follow-state-true spells its sibling — and afterwards there is still exactly one row: output.size() == 1 for favorite?user_id=eq.…&article_id=eq.…. The refusal is reachable rather than theoretical, and the path matters: the Caddyfile injects Prefer: resolution=ignore-duplicates, which targets the primary key — a client-minted uuid that never collides — so a repeated POST reaches uq_favorite_pair and comes back 23505 instead of being merged away. In practice only a double press inside the optimistic window gets there, and it reads "You've already favorited this one." The regression this catches is the tempting simplification of dropping that index because "the button already knows": the button is optimistic, and the index is the only thing that makes the count a fact.

Saving a piece puts it on the reader's list at once, and pressing again takes it off. The insert carries no user and no date — output.user_id == input.auth_uid && output.created_at != null — because both are column defaults, and the star fills on the press rather than after the write lands: the per-card probe is a local read over rows RLS has already narrowed to mine. The retraction is the filtered sibling (article_id=eq.{id}), so afterwards output.size() == 0 for that pair. What this test also asserts is a negative, and it is the one worth catching: nothing else on the card moves. A save writes no count and feeds no sink, so no other reader's screen changes and no number anywhere is a function of who saved what (decision 35). The regression it guards is the plausible-sounding "while we are here, show a save count" — which would make a private intention public and change what people save.

A person may save a piece at most once, however many times they press: error.kind == 'unique_violation' && error.field == 'uq_bookmark_pair', and afterwards exactly one row remains. The path is the one test-favorite-once documents, and the reason it is repeated here rather than assumed is that it depends on a property of the request and not of the table: Caddy injects resolution=ignore-duplicates on every POST, PostgREST aims that at the primary key, and a client-minted uuid never collides — so the composite index is what answers. A new owned table that inherits the Caddy header without inheriting a composite unique silently merges duplicates instead of refusing them.

The reading-list read names no reader and still returns exactly one reader's saves: output.all(r, r.user_id == input.auth_uid) under a token, and a second person's identical request against bookmark?order=created_at.desc&limit=20 returns their own list and not the first person's. Bookmark is owned, so the SELECT policy is the filter — which is what makes the whole screen safe to write with no user_id anywhere in its markup. There is no derived face to leak through either: unlike a favorite, a save feeds no sink, so there is no second table where the pair could surface under weaker access. The regression this catches is somebody "fixing" the read by adding an explicit user_id=eq.… filter from the session, which would look more careful and would move the guarantee from the database into the client.

Favorite is owned, and the per-card probe is exact because RLS has already narrowed it: read under a person's token it returns only their own rows — output.all(f, f.user_id == input.user_id) — and a second person's favorite of the same article is invisible to the first (output.all(f, f.id != input.other_favorite_id)) even though the two see the identical count. That split is the design: the hot, per-card, "is it mine" answer is private and local, and the public faces of a favorite are derived (ArticleStats, FavoriteIndex). The regression this catches is widening the SELECT arm in raw SQL to make a profile's favorites readable: the browser re-applies the declared access mode on every collection read, so the same table would mean "mine" on the local path and "everyone's" on the server path — a difference decided by which path a regex happened to take (decision 2).

Follow

Nobody can follow themselves, however the write arrives. The row invariant this.follower_id != this.followed_id is a table CHECK, so a hand-made insert is refused at the database — error.kind == 'check_violation' && output == null — and no surface emits one anyway: on one's own profile the Me probe is non-empty (output.mine.size() == 1) and CSS swaps the follow control for Edit your profile, and on one's own piece the byline shows Edit and Delete instead. All three are checked, because the visible arm and the database arm fail independently: a CSS regression that reveals the button must still not produce a row, and a dropped CHECK must still not be reachable through the UI.

A follow button reads its true state on every screen it appears on, including on a fresh visit after signing back in, because the state is read rather than remembered: Follow is owned on follower_id, so the probe follow?followed_id=eq.… returns exactly this reader's own rows — output.all(f, f.follower_id == input.user_id) && output.size() == (input.follows_them ? 1 : 0) — and it resolves in-tab against the synced collection, so a fresh visit paints the correct arm without a round trip. The same predicate holds on the profile header (followed_id=eq.{id}) and on the article byline (followed_id=eq.{author_id}), which is what "on every screen it appears on" means concretely. Following the same person twice is refused — error.kind == 'unique_violation' under uq_follow_pair — so the state is never two rows deep and unfollowing can be a single row-scoped delete.

Given a reader following B and not C, the feed read returns exactly B's writing and nothing else: output.size() == input.b_articles.size() && output.all(a, a.author_id == input.b_id) — no filter names the reader, because nothing can: follow!inner sees only this reader's rows by RLS, the inner-ness propagates up through app_user!inner, and every article whose author they do not follow is dropped. Following C fills the feed without a navigation — output.exists(a, a.author_id == input.c_id) — and unfollowing takes it back out — output.all(a, a.author_id != input.c_id) — because follow is in the region's dependency set and the collection is the change signal. With no follows at all the read is empty and error-free (output == [] && error == null) and the screen renders its designed empty state, which tells the reader how to fill it rather than showing a blank list. Two things make this the first read to verify live at hop 3: a PGRST201 here means the hint did not resolve the ambiguity, and a result set that includes C's writing means the inner-ness did not propagate — both fail silently in the UI, which is why the size and the membership are both pinned above. The embed names its relationship, follow!followed_id!inner(follower_id), because Follow carries a foreign key on both of its columns; the region still watches follow, since embedTables matches a repeating hint run — which is what makes “fills the feed without a navigation” true and is covered by its own regression assertion in interpreter/pending-smoke.js. The same read continues onto feed-older unchanged but for the cursor, so paging out of a feed cannot silently widen it to the community.

favorite-recount

Given a favorite table where article 'A' has two rows from two different people and article 'B' has one, the transform emits one absolute row per article: output.exists(r, r.article_id == 'A' && r.favorite_count == 2) && output.exists(r, r.article_id == 'B' && r.favorite_count == 1). The count is a pure function of the table's current contents and the triggering event's key, never of a delta, so one person's favorite shows in another person's count without a reload and the number is the same for everyone — the sink is public-read and no reader's token narrows it. Idempotence: replaying the same event, or any unrelated crud-table event, recomputes identical rows and upserts them on_conflict=article_id with resolution=merge-duplicates, so output == input.previous_output and at-least-once delivery is indistinguishable from exactly-once; out-of-order delivery converges because no value depends on arrival order.

The un-favorite-to-zero boundary, which is the one this pipeline gets wrong if anybody simplifies it. With article 'A' holding exactly one favorite, deleting it fires a CDC event whose back-filled before-image carries 'A'; the absolute re-read comes back without that article, and the union of the event's own key is what makes the transform emit the zero row anyway: output.exists(r, r.article_id == 'A' && r.favorite_count == 0). Without the union no row is emitted for A, the upsert writes nothing, and the count freezes at 1 forever while the pill reads unset — a visibly wrong number that no other test would catch. Replaying the delete emits the identical zero row. The same path carries an article deletion, whose cascaded favorite DELETEs each drive their article's count to zero, and it is why ArticleStats deliberately carries no foreign key to Article: that final POST names a row that no longer exists, and a FK would reject it, retry three times and wedge the stream on every deletion.

The monoid law the optimistic projection rests on, executed rather than asserted in a comment. Folding a set of favorites whole must equal folding any two halves separately and combining them: result(fold(rows)) == result(combine(fold(left), fold(right))). This is not a property of counting in general — it is the reason decision 37 may show a total the sink has never held, by combining the stored accumulator with a fold over this session's uncounted rows. Break associativity, or make empty anything but the identity, and the number on screen stops agreeing with the number the pipeline will eventually write — silently, and only for readers who have just written something.

favorite-index

The cross-user view a screen read cannot state: one active row per current favorite, keyed by the pair, materialised with the service token that sees every row regardless of RLS. output.exists(r, r.id == input.user_id + ':' + input.article_id && r.active == true && r.favorited_at == input.created_at), and every emitted object carries the same five keys — output.all(r, has(r.id) && has(r.user_id) && has(r.article_id) && has(r.active) && has(r.favorited_at)) — because PostgREST rejects a bulk insert whose objects disagree on their key set, which is why an inactive row repeats the pair's own columns rather than being a partial patch. Idempotence: absolute over favorite, upserted on_conflict=id with resolution=merge-duplicates; replaying any event recomputes the same rows, and the sink grows in pairs ever favorited, never in events delivered.

An upsert-only sink can never delete, so a retraction has to be representable as a value. Un-favoriting emits the same key with output.exists(r, r.id == input.user_id + ':' + input.article_id && r.active == false), and the profile read filters active=is.true, so the piece leaves that person's favorited list rather than being orphaned there: output.favorited.all(r, r.article_id != input.article_id). Deleting an article travels the identical path — its cascaded favorite DELETEs each retract their pair — which is what keeps a removed piece out of every profile it was favorited on. Re-favoriting the same pair flips it back to active in the same absolute pass. The regression this catches is an optimisation that emits only the grouped rows: it is correct on every case except the one that matters.

tag-recount

The popular tags panel counts live articles, and it counts them by set cardinality rather than by row count. Given article_tag holding 'dragons' on two articles and 'training' on one, the transform emits output.exists(r, r.id == 'dragons' && r.article_count == 2) && output.exists(r, r.id == 'training' && r.article_count == 1) — distinct article ids per tag, so a duplicate pair (impossible under uq_article_tag_pair, and this is the assertion that keeps it so) could not inflate a count. Publishing, retagging and deleting all arrive as ordinary article_tag INSERT and DELETE events — the trigger's delete-and-reinsert and the FK cascade — so the counts stay honest through all three without a special case. Idempotence: absolute over article_tag, upserted on_conflict=id with resolution=merge-duplicates; replaying any event recomputes every tag's count from scratch.

The last article to shed a tag drives its count to zero rather than leaving it at one. With 'dragons' on exactly one article, retagging that article without the word deletes the pair; the re-read comes back without the tag, and the union of the event's own tag emits output.exists(r, r.id == 'dragons' && r.article_count == 0). The panel's read then drops the chip rather than showing a dead one, because the filter is article_count=gt.0: output.panel.all(r, r.article_count > 0) && output.panel.all(r, r.id != 'dragons'). Deleting the article instead of retagging it takes the identical path through the FK cascade. A reader who follows the tag's own link anyway finds a screen that says so plainly rather than an empty list.

ArticleStats

The count is one public number, not a per-reader one: the sink is public-read and written only by its pipeline, so two people reading the same card get the same value — output.a.favorite_count == output.b.favorite_count — and a favorite by one moves the number on the other's open screen without a reload, carried by the live path. An article nobody has favorited has no sink row at all (a per-subject sink's row appears with its first event, and there is no seed), and the card must still read zero rather than blank: output.rendered.favorite_count == 0 via data-empty-row='{"favorite_count":0}'. The stored value is never negative — this >= 0 — which is the assertion that would catch any drift from absolute recomputation toward delta arithmetic. The set arm is asserted separately, because it is the arm a reader presses first and the one a nesting mistake silently breaks: on a card this reader has already favorited, the filled pill shows the same public number as the outline one would — output.rendered.set_arm.favorite_count == output.rendered.unset_arm.favorite_count and output.rendered.set_arm.favorite_count == output.a.favorite_count. Both arms are siblings of the favorite probe, so both bind article_id=eq.{id} against the article row; nested inside the probe the set arm would bind against the favorite row, match nothing, and render 0 on precisely the pieces this reader has favorited (decision 23).

home

Someone who has just come in and follows nobody still sees the whole community's writing: the community read carries no follow condition and no reader-scoped filter, so with three articles by three people and zero follows output.size() == 3 — never a blank page. Newest first is quantified over every adjacent pair: range(output.size() - 1).all(i, output[i].created_at >= output[i + 1].created_at). When the community really is empty the region renders its sentence rather than nothing (output == [] && error == null): "Nothing has been written here yet. Yours could be the first."

Every preview is the same six parts wherever it is listed, and the read is what guarantees the parts exist: each row of the community, feed, older, tag, search and profile lists carries its author embedded rather than copied — output.all(a, has(a.author.handle) && a.author.handle.size() > 0 && has(a.created_at) && a.title.size() > 0) — with the tags coming from a nested ArticleTag region keyed on the row and the count from a nested ArticleStats region, so output.all(a, has(a.tags) && has(a.favorite_count)) holds on every one of those six screens from identical markup. A display name is nullable and a picture is optional, and neither may become a hole: with {display_name: null, image_url: null} the byline still reads output.rendered.byline == input.author.handle and the avatar is the quiet circle, achieved by CSS on empty content rather than by a conditional the markup does not have.

A second person signing in sees the same community writing — output.b.community == output.a.community, the same rows in the same order, because nothing about that read is reader-scoped — while everything that belongs to a person is their own: their feed follows their own follows (output.b.feed.all(a, output.b.follows.exists(f, f.followed_id == a.author_id))), their favorites are invisible to the first (output.a.favorites.all(f, f.user_id == input.a_id)), and their profile and settings resolve through their own Me row. The counts they both see are equal (test-stats-shared-and-zero). This is the multi-user pass the verify loop runs with two tokens, and it is the one that catches an accidentally reader-scoped community read.

Every screen reads correctly in both the device's light and dark appearance: each DESIGN.md token carries a light and a dark value (input.tokens.all(t, t.light != '' && t.dark != '')), screens style their states through tokens only and declare no literal hex (input.screen_css.all(r, !r.matches('#[0-9a-fA-F]{3,8}'))), and switching the appearance re-resolves the tokens with no state change and no reload. Type carries the same kind of rule, because the serif/sans duality is half of this identity and prose alone cannot fail a lint: a screen stylesheet sets family and size only through the five role classes and never inline — input.screen_css.all(r, (r.declares_font ? r.selector.startsWith('.role-') : true)) — and every role named in the Screens intro's table resolves to a DESIGN.md role (input.roles.all(t, t in input.tokens.typography)). Storyboarded as each screen's populated-dark frame and completed to every frame in the verify pass; the body's own rendered vocabulary — headings, quote rule, code ground, link underline — is included, since that is the surface the identity was chosen for.

older

A long list gives a screenful and a plain way to more. With twenty-five articles the community read returns output.size() == 20, and the link under the twentieth card carries that card's own created_at as the cursor — the number that would otherwise have to be computed is a value the row already holds, which is how paging exists at all in a markup with no arithmetic. The next page is exactly the remainder, with nothing repeated and nothing skipped: output.size() == 5 && output.all(a, a.created_at < input.when) && output.all(a, !input.first_page.exists(b, b.id == a.id)), still newest first (range(output.size() - 1).all(i, output[i].created_at >= output[i + 1].created_at)). Each page carries the same link again, so there is no last page and no ceiling — but only where a full screenful came back: the link is revealed by :nth-child(20), so with nineteen articles no link is offered at all (output.size() < 20 ? output.paging_link == null : output.paging_link != null), which is what keeps a young community from being handed a dead end. Both pages order created_at.desc,id.desc, so the sequence is total and the two reads agree about it. Reaching the beginning returns the empty collection without erroring (output == [] && error == null) and the screen says so with a way back. The remaining boundary is an accepted loss recorded in decision 20 rather than a to-do parked here — rows sharing the cursor's own timestamp exactly are dropped by the strict lt., because one :param cannot carry a composite cursor. What this paragraph does verify live at hop 3 is the round trip: the timestamptz survives interpolation into the href, the browser's encoding, the router's decoding and the filter's re-encoding unchanged. And feed pages to feed-older, whose read carries the follow embed, so page two of a feed is still a feed: output.all(a, input.follows.exists(f, f.followed_id == a.author_id)).

article

An article's body renders as the markdown it was written in. Given a body holding a heading, emphasis, a link, a list, a quote and a fenced block, the terminal's renderer produces elements rather than characters: output.elements.exists(e, e.tag == 'h2') && output.elements.exists(e, e.tag == 'strong') && output.elements.exists(e, e.tag == 'em') && output.elements.exists(e, e.tag == 'a') && output.elements.exists(e, e.tag == 'ul') && output.elements.exists(e, e.tag == 'blockquote') && output.elements.exists(e, e.tag == 'pre') — all seven, from one binding with data-text-format="markdown" and no app code. It is read at a comfortable measure rather than the full width of the window: output.measure_ch >= 60 && output.measure_ch <= 70 (the screen's private --measure: 65ch), and wide code scrolls inside its own box so the page never scrolls sideways. The vocabulary the renderer does not carry — tables, nested lists, reference links — is out of scope in the brief too, so the subset and the promise agree exactly; a body using one of them reads as its literal characters rather than failing.

A body containing HTML renders as the characters the writer typed, and never as working markup. For {body: '<script>alert(1)</script> <b>bold?</b>'} the store keeps it verbatim (output.body == input.body) and the rendered result contains those characters as text with no element of either kind: output.text.contains('<script>') && output.elements.all(e, e.tag != 'script' && e.tag != 'b'). A link whose address is not a web address is not a link: {body: '[click](javascript:alert(1))'} renders output.text.contains('click') && output.elements.all(e, e.tag != 'a'), while an ordinary https: link does become an anchor (output.elements.exists(e, e.tag == 'a')) — the allowlist is http, https, mailto and relative, and the negative case alone would pass trivially if the renderer emitted no anchors at all, which is why both are asserted. This holds by construction rather than by filtering: the renderer builds every node with createElement and textContent and never touches innerHTML, so there is no sanitizer to keep current and no bypass to find. The regression it catches is any future "just this once" innerHTML in the terminal's text dispatch.

Only an article's author is offered edit and delete on it; another person's article offers follow and favorite instead — and the contrast is exact, not approximate. On one's own piece the Me probe is non-empty and the reader arm is hidden: output.controls == ['edit', 'delete', 'count']; on somebody else's it is empty and the owner arm is hidden: output.controls == ['follow', 'favorite']. Both arms are always in the markup and CSS chooses between them, so there is no conditional rendering and no flash of the wrong one after the first refresh — the loading state hides the arms outright, which is what the loading frame draws. The favorite pill is outside the swap and appears in both lists above: it is the same pressable control for the author and for everybody else, because it is the same control on every list screen, none of which carries a me probe on its cards — output.owner.controls.exists(c, c == 'favorite') && output.reader.controls.exists(c, c == 'favorite'). The regression this catches is an owner-only static chip: it withholds a control nothing forbids, and it makes one article carry two different affordances depending on which screen the reader is standing on, which is exactly what accept-preview-complete exists to prevent. Delete, by contrast, is deliberately two gestures — the <details> summary arms it and the inner submit commits it — so output.owner.delete_requires_arming == true.

editor

The editor is rebuilt on every visit rather than held (input.keep == 0), so the sheet is blank every time and a piece being revised is read fresh: output.fields.all(f, f.value == '') on arrival at /editor. Its own top-level region is the reader's Me row, which always has exactly one row (output.size() == 1), so the screen is never in empty and the writing surface can never be replaced by an empty state — the failure this catches is a top-level region whose emptiness would blank a form the person is typing into.

settings

/settings carries no route param and still knows who is asking: its top-level region is Me, and the profile form sits inside a nested AppUser region filtered on that row's id, so the row under the form is always the reader's own — output.form_row.id == input.user_id — and no hidden id field is needed or offered. The fields arrive filled from the row rather than blank (output.display_name_value == input.display_name), so saving one field does not clear the others. The screen draws no sign-out button: the terminal owns sign-out and puts it in the chrome beside the handle, and a second copy would be an unreviewed duplicate of a platform affordance.

profile

A person's profile shows who they are and both of their lists. The header read resolves the handle to exactly one row — output.size() == 1 && output[0].handle == input.handle — carrying picture, handle, display name and about, with the name and picture optional and neither leaving a hole. Their writing is exactly their own, newest first: output.articles.all(a, a.author.handle == input.handle) && range(output.articles.size() - 1).all(i, output.articles[i].created_at >= output.articles[i + 1].created_at). The ones they have favorited are a route away, read from the derived FavoriteIndex because Favorite is private, filtered to that person and to live retractions and ordered by when they favorited: output.favorited.all(r, r.user.handle == input.handle && r.active == true) && range(output.favorited.size() - 1).all(i, output.favorited[i].favorited_at >= output.favorited[i + 1].favorited_at). Each favorited row nests a live read of the article itself, so its title and byline are always current rather than a copy taken when it was favorited: output.favorited.all(r, r.article.title == input.articles[r.article_id].title). A handle nobody goes by returns the empty header and the empty list, each with its own sentence, rather than a blank page.

tag

With two articles carrying 'dragons', one carrying only 'training', and one carrying no tags at all, the tag screen's inner-embed read returns exactly the first two — output.size() == 2 && output.all(a, input.tags[a.id].exists(t, t == 'dragons')) — newest first, in the same preview cards as every other list. The route param round-trips: a multi-word tag is URI-encoded into the filter and interpolated back into the heading, so output.heading == '#' + input.name reads as the word the writer typed.

A tag with nothing live under it says so plainly rather than showing an empty list: output == [] && error == null, and the screen's designed empty state carries the words and a way onward ("Nothing is filed under #dragons yet." / "A tag exists because an article carries it"). The route is reachable in two honest ways — a stale link, and a tag whose last article was retagged or deleted between the panel rendering and the tap — so this is a state a person really reaches, not a defensive branch.

search

Searching words from an article's title, description or body finds it, because the generated tsvector concatenates all three before vectorizing. Given one article titled 'Market run', one whose description holds 'market', and one whose body holds it, the read for {q: 'market'} returns all three: output.size() == 3. Multi-word queries are taken as plain words — {q: 'market run'} finds the titled piece (output.exists(a, a.title == 'Market run')) — because the operator is plfts(simple): plainto_tsquery, never tsquery syntax, where a stray operator would error the whole read into network-error instead of returning nothing. The box appears on every reading screen, which is what "from anywhere" means, and is deliberately absent from the three writing screens where a stray submit would navigate away from unsent text.

A search matching nothing says so plainly: the storyboard's own two-word {q: 'attic keys'} returns output == [] && error == null and the screen renders its designed empty frame with the query echoed back and a way onward — not a blank, and not network-error. The distinction is the point: an empty result and a failed read must not look the same to a reader, and the simple text-search configuration is what keeps a nonsense query a result rather than an error.

Decisions

  1. Conduit takes the terminal's door and therefore has no anonymous reader — the one place this app deliberately departs from the RealWorld specification. RealWorld specifies email/password registration with a user-chosen username and a JWT the app manages. The terminal publishes its offer as data — capabilities.auth.modes: ["passkey", "guest"], identity: "generated" (plugins/omnishell/terminal.cue:56-65) — and #emit refuses any other mode, so an email/password program does not compile. The gate is also binary: shell.js:254-266 checks auth.required before a store is created or a screen is mounted, renders its own login chrome when no session exists, and with required: false issues no Authorization header at all — under which anon holds schema USAGE and no table privileges (emit.cue:603,614), so an ungated Conduit would read nothing. There is no anonymous-read-with-optional-sign-in mode to ask for. Two designs were therefore rejected outright: a hand-written sign-in screen with an email field, and a public browse mode with a sign-in prompt on write. Per the prelude's capability doctrine, demanding what a runtime does not offer is a compile error; the brief already names this in Out of scope ("the door is the platform's and it comes first") and the identity section spells the ceremony out. Accepted losses, recorded rather than smoothed over: a search-engine crawler sees the login chrome and nothing else, a shared article link costs one guest click before it reads, and a guest identity lives in sessionStorage — closing the tab ends it, and only a passkey brings a person back to their own articles (accept-signout-return). What the app gets in exchange is the whole ceremony for free: one tap, no typing, a unique generated handle, the name · handle · sign out chrome, the nav, and 401 recovery, none of it designed here.
  2. Public content with owner writes is public-read plus additive raw policies — never a declared owned widened in SQL. #Access has four modes and none of them is "everyone reads, the owner writes". public-read emits exactly a SELECT arm for app_user and the service catch-all (emit.cue:172-177), so a public-read table whose rows come from forms is unbuildable as declared: every insert is refused. 011_owner_writes.sql adds the missing arms — INSERT/UPDATE/DELETE on article gated author_id = auth_uid(), INSERT/DELETE on comment, UPDATE on app_user gated id = auth_uid() — and drops nothing, because public-read put nothing there. The rejected alternative was declaring owned and widening SELECT in raw SQL. It produces correct server behaviour and a booby trap: emit.cue:305 mirrors the declared mode into shell.yaml, and visible() (data-crud.js:174-200) re-applies it in the browser on every collection read, knowing only the four declared modes — so the same table would mean "mine" on a locally-translated read and "everyone's" on a server-computed one, with a regex and a probe row deciding which. The rule this compile adopts: the declared mode is the mode the client must believe; widen only in the direction the mirror already allows, narrow freely. One column narrowing follows from the same rule, and it is load-bearing rather than tidy: an RLS policy gates which row may be updated and never which columns, and 002_grants.sql emits table-wide GRANT SELECT, INSERT, UPDATE, DELETE ON ALL TABLES … TO app_user (emit.cue:612-618), so 011's USING (id = auth_uid()) would happily let a hand-made PATCH /crud/app_user?id=eq.<me> {"handle":"…"} through. 011 therefore also carries REVOKE UPDATE ON app_user FROM app_user; GRANT UPDATE (display_name, bio, image_url) ON app_user TO app_user; — narrowing, so safe under this entry's own rule, and PostgREST honours column privileges with 42501. Without it the brief's “it never changes” is untrue, and the whole of Me.handle's “cannot drift” justification — the thing that makes the denormalisation safe — rests on nothing. Two deliberate absences follow. comment gets no UPDATE arm — the brief puts editing a comment out of scope and the missing policy is what keeps that true even if a form ever appears — and article_tag gets no arm at all, because every row of it is the trigger's (decision 10). Backend charter: access models are this seat's, and both files carry the argument in their header comments.
  3. Me is a table the brief never names, and it is the most load-bearing thing in the design. The interpreter has no session binding: lookup (screen.js:157) resolves {param.x} and dot paths into the current row and nothing else — there is no {user.id}, and a screen therefore cannot ask "is this mine?". Conduit asks four times: edit and delete on your own article (accept-author-only-controls), the × on your own comment (accept-comment-remove), "Edit your profile" instead of Follow on your own page (accept-no-self-follow), and a /settings route with no :param to identify you with. Me answers all four the only way the platform allows: one row per person, owned on id, so RLS has already decided what the reader may see and the row's mere visibility, joined against an author_id or a handle, is the answer. All four reads are col=eq.<v> with no select, so they run in-tab against the synced collection — no round trip per card. Written by a trigger (014_me_provision.sql) rather than a pipeline because the row must exist on the first paint after a one-click guest sign-in and a CDC round trip is seconds; SECURITY DEFINER because the auth service inserts app_user under the service role. 012_me_readonly.sql then drops the INSERT/UPDATE/DELETE arms owned emits, which is narrowing and therefore always safe: nothing should write this table from a browser, and a person deleting their own row would lock themselves out of their own settings screen. Rejected: duplicating the reader's uuid into every table as a denormalised is_mine-style column (a lie the moment two people read the same row), and a per-card server read joining app_user to itself (correct, one round trip per card, re-fetched on every dependency change).
  4. A probe region's :empty state is the ownership and toggle primitive; both arms are always in the markup and CSS chooses. A region refresh sweeps the region down to exactly its current row nodes and removes the <template data-item> on the first pass (screen.js:803-807), and the <p class="empty"> is appended only when data-empty is set (screen.js:821-826). So for a probe carrying no data-empty, region has rows ⇔ region:not(:empty) — a pure-CSS reading of an RLS answer, with no session placeholder, no conditional rendering (there is no data-if) and no second read. Every owner-only control and both toggles are built from it: .probe:empty ~ .when-set { display: none } and its inverse — a sibling combinator, which already tells you that the control cannot be inside the probe. Three rules the assembly must keep, and the first is now stated per probe shape, because one universal was wrong in one direction and impossible in the other. An ownership probe's control is a plain sibling: the comment × sits beside the comment row's me probe, wired against the enclosing comment (test-comment-remove states it correctly). A toggle probe's retraction is a sibling carrying data-filter: a bare sibling would call store.remove("favorite", <articleId>) (wireForm, screen.js:778) and delete nothing at all, while a child would bind every nested region inside it — the count included — against the favorite row rather than the article (syncNested → hydrateRegion(el, entry.ctx, false), screen.js:736-745). The filtered form is the platform's own mechanism for exactly this: removeWhere resolves the matching ids with a GET under the reader's own token, so RLS scopes the delete to my own row without the markup ever naming me (data-crud.js:339-343, SPEC #Form.filter). So article_id=eq.{id} retracts a favorite and followed_id=eq.{id} (profile header) or followed_id=eq.{author_id} (article byline) retracts a follow, and both pill arms bind against the article row, which is what makes the one declared ArticleStats read correct in both. And every screen carrying a probe writes [data-state="loading"] .arms { visibility: hidden }, because before the first refresh the <template> is still a child and the region reads :not(:empty) for one frame. Rejected: a click handler reading a session — handlers fire only on drag (screen.js:827-830) and are denied window, document and fetch by check-handlers.ts, so this is not a thing the platform can be asked for.
  5. The right control is offered, and the server refuses the wrong write anyway — both, in that order. accept-author-only-controls demands a visible contrast ("only an article's author is offered edit and delete; another person's article offers follow and favorite instead"), so visibility rides the Me probe of decision 4. But visibility is not enforcement: a hand-typed /editor/<someone-else's-id> renders, and its save matches no policy in 011, so PostgREST answers 4xx, data-crud.js raises a NonRetriableError, and refused (screen.js:589-592) unhides .store-error and sets validation-error — the reader is told "This piece isn't yours to change." in --danger, with nothing saved. That refusal is storyboarded as edit-article-validation-error, deliberately, because it is this app's headline behaviour and the brief asks for it in those words: "Conduit refuses the rest rather than hiding the button". Rejected: hiding the route (nothing can hide a hash), and trusting the probe alone (a CSS rule is not a security boundary and this ir will not pretend otherwise).
  6. Favorite is private; its two public faces are derived tables. The pill must answer "have I favorited this" on every card of every list, on a fresh visit, without a round trip — so Favorite is owned on user_id and the probe read favorite?article_id=eq.{id} is in the locally translatable subset, resolving in-tab against the synced collection and flipping optimistically on the press. That privacy costs two public facts, and each is bought back by a pipeline rather than by weakening the mode: the count everyone sees is ArticleStats via favorite-recount, and another person's favorited list — required by accept-profile-page — is FavoriteIndex via favorite-index, whose active boolean is how an upsert-only sink retracts an un-favorite. The pipeline-designer card fired on both, and two of its three rules hold: the value is read on every render of a list screen (hot path), and more than one surface consumes it. Rejected: making Favorite public-read and answering the toggle with a per-card embed through me — correct, but one server round trip per card re-issued on every dependency change; and a trigger-maintained public mirror — exact and instant, but a third trigger where the platform already publishes an answer for cross-user views. Two accepted losses, both named rather than left to be measured. The list lags a favorite by pipeline latency (seconds); no acceptance id demands otherwise, and the reader's own pill on those cards is optimistic regardless. And the card itself costs a round trip: each favorited row nests a live Article read whose aliased embed parseSelect rejects (data-crud.js:109-114), so the region is server-computed — up to twenty GETs per paint, re-fired whenever article or app_user changes, and app_user changes whenever anybody saves their settings. It is bounded by the list's own limit=20 and it is the price of the preview being the live row: denormalising title, description and byline into FavoriteIndex would remove the fetch and break accept-edit-propagates and accept-profile-propagates for this one list, which is a worse trade than a bounded fetch on the app's least-travelled screen. decision 13 is the other half of this: without a foreign key on article_id there is no single embedded-filter read that could replace the twenty.
  7. Your Feed is one read, and it names no reader — RLS supplies the identity the binding grammar cannot. The read is article?select=*,author:app_user!inner(handle,display_name,image_url,follow!inner(follower_id))&order=created_at.desc&limit=20. Two measurements make it work (probed live against a running mecha cluster on 2026-08-02): an !inner embed drops parents whose child set is empty, and the inner-ness propagates up through a second level (M1); and RLS decides what an embedded resource can see, so follow!inner sees only rows where follower_id = auth_uid() (M2). Together: every article whose author I do not follow has an empty follow embed, fails the inner join, and is dropped — with no filter naming me, because nothing can name me. embedTables (data-crud.js:100-101) puts both app_user and follow in the region's dependency set, so a new follow fills the feed without a navigation (accept-follow-fills-feed). Rejected: a feed sink materialised by a pipeline (a whole entity and pipeline, seconds of lag on a follow, and the brief says "immediately"); a denormalised follower_ids text column on Article (no array type, and a second definition of who follows whom); and client-side filtering (there is no client-side computation). The belt-and-braces fallback, if the inner-ness ever fails to propagate against Conduit's own constraint graph, is an explicit embedded filter &author.follow.follower_id=not.is.null — recorded here because it must be a decision, not a debugging discovery. QA charter: this read is the first thing hop 3 verifies against the running cluster with an app_user token.
  8. Follow carries foreign keys on both of its columns, and the feed read names its relationship — the platform was extended rather than the schema contorted. Two foreign keys from follow to app_user makes the embed ambiguous and PostgREST answers 300 PGRST201 naming every candidate relationship (measured directly: two throwaway tables with two foreign keys to app_user were created in a dev database, probed, and dropped). The documented cure is a hint — follow!followed_id!inner(…) — and at the time the data design was written that hint broke the interpreter: embedTables matched at most one !hint and captured followed_id as the table name, dropping follow from the feed region's dependency set and making the feed deaf to a new follow (M4). An earlier revision of this design bought its way out by dropping the second foreign key, which removed the ambiguity at its root and kept the read unhinted — at the cost of referential integrity and ON DELETE CASCADE on the column that points at a person. That interpreter defect is fixed: the character class was made repeating, (?:![a-z_][a-z0-9_]*)* (plugins/omnishell/interpreter/data-crud.js:101), with a regression assertion in interpreter/pending-smoke.js citing accept-follow-fills-feed. The fix was measured directly on the fragment this app needs — "*,author:app_user!inner(handle,follow!followed_id!inner(follower_id))" went from ["app_user", "followed_id"] (deaf) to ["app_user", "follow"]. So this revision takes the shape the prelude prefers: both columns carry REFERENCES app_user(id) ON DELETE CASCADE, and feed and feed-older read *,author:app_user!inner(handle,display_name,image_url,follow!followed_id!inner(follower_id)). The hint is the FK column name rather than the constraint name, because the column name does not depend on what Postgres decided to call the constraint. Extending the terminal beat contorting the schema, which is the doctrine working rather than a principle admired; the schema keeps its integrity, an account-deletion feature would cascade correctly if it ever ships, and the one residual risk is that a PGRST201 renders as an empty region rather than an error — which is why this read is the first thing hop 3 runs live with an app_user token (decision 7).
  9. The readable address is the title plus the first hex group of the row's own uuid. accept-slug-readable asks for an address derived from the title that still tells two same-titled articles apart. A generated column takes IMMUTABLE expressions over the row's own columns only — no subqueries, no other rows, no now(), and notably no loop for a free numeric suffix — so the row's own identity is the only discriminator available: coalesce(nullif(btrim(left(regexp_replace(lower(title), '[^a-z0-9]+', '-', 'g'), 80), '-'), ''), 'article') || '-' || left(id::text, 8), STORED UNIQUE. Measured in Postgres: how-to-train-your-dragon-4f5df9a9 and how-to-train-your-dragon-7f2af670 for two articles titled How to Train Your Dragon!, and article-469615a1 for a title of pure punctuation. UNIQUE is kept: it costs an index the /article/:slug read wants anyway and it is the backstop for the ~1-in-10⁸ collision, which reaches the writer as a readable .store-error rather than a corrupted route. Two consequences owned rather than discovered: the column is GENERATED ALWAYS, so retitling changes the address and an old link 404s (which is why the edit route is keyed on the id — decision 22); and the address carries eight characters no human chose, the price of "readable and unique" without a uniquifying loop. Rejected: a client-minted slug field (two writers, one word, and a race), and a trigger that loops for a free suffix (a third trigger, and a write that can fail on contention).
  10. ArticleTag is a trigger's output, not a pipeline's — the one place this design refuses the recount discipline, and why. The platform has no array type and one form writes one row, so Article.tags is a single text column: the words as the writer typed them, round-tripped by the editor. Three things need tag rows: the /tag/:name inner-embed list, the chips on every preview (a nested region cannot split a string), and tag-recount, which needs a crud-path source because the publication covers crud tables only. A pipeline cannot produce them: an absolute recount can re-assert the pairs an article has, but the CDC event carries the after-image only, so it can never retract the pair it no longer has — retagging would leave stale rows and a dishonest popular-tags panel, contradicting accept-tagcount-true. 013_article_tag_sync.sql is delete-and-reinsert inside the article's own transaction, which is exact by construction and makes the piece appear on each tag's list the moment it appears on the community list (accept-tags-file). SECURITY DEFINER because article_tag carries no app_user write policy at all; deletion needs no arm because the FK cascades. The IS NOT DISTINCT FROM guard is not decoration: the editor's update form always names tags (AFTER UPDATE OF fires on the column being named, not changed), so without it every title fix would churn the whole tag set, replace every chip's DOM node and wake the pipeline for nothing. The corollary the form contract must keep: the revise form always submits all four fields, because omitting tags would silently freeze the projection.
  11. Every derived count is an absolute recount over the source table, never a delta — and every mapping unions the triggering event's own key. All three pipelines are cdc, and the emitted shape (emit.cue:190-245) is a redis-streams input, a branch that GETs ${CRUD_URL}/<source>?<aggregate> with the service token, one bloblang, and a retrying POST to ${CRUD_URL}/<sink>?on_conflict=<pk> carrying Prefer: resolution=merge-duplicates. The CDC event is a trigger, never a payload: the emitted rows are a pure function of the source table's current contents, so replaying an event — or any other event, since every cdc pipeline reads the whole cdc-events stream and from is documentation rather than a filter — recomputes the same values and overwrites them identically. At-least-once delivery is therefore indistinguishable from exactly-once, and out-of-order delivery converges because no value depends on arrival order. Deltas were rejected for the reason the prelude states: at-least-once means a delta applied twice is simply wrong, and nothing in the transport can promise otherwise. The one subtlety, and it is load-bearing: an absolute read over the source cannot see a subject that has just lost its last row, so each mapping unions the event row's own key into the emitted set — without it, the last un-favorite leaves the count frozen at 1 forever, contradicting accept-favorite-toggles. That depends on conduit's restore-deleted-row processor back-filling Payload.After from Payload.Before on deletes, which is present in this tree (emit.cue:745-760). Each mapping also throws rather than degrade when the harness hands it a bare array at the root, because a groups-only count would silently leave stale finals behind. The second subtlety is the cost of that shared stream, and it is the reason each mapping's guard drops the message rather than reasserting the truth. Six tables are in the publication; resolution=merge-duplicates is ON CONFLICT DO UPDATE, which fires the emitted txid restamp trigger (emit.cue:677-696) on every row it touches; so a no-op reassertion would give every article_stats, favorite_index and tag_count row a fresh WAL version on every unrelated comment, and Electric would fan the whole sink out to every connected tab. The write and sync amplification is O(sink) per unrelated event, which is not harmlessness. root = deleted() on a foreign event costs one GET and no write, and the discriminator is exact per pipeline: user_id occurs only on favorite among the crud tables (article and comment carry author_id, follow carries follower_id), and tag only on article_tag (article carries tags). The maintenance trap is stated where it will be needed: a future crud table carrying one of those column names silently re-opens the storm. pipeline-designer fired here on its "insert, update and delete must all be handled" clause.
  12. A sink read as a list keys on id; a sink read only as a nested singleton keys on its subject. A list region keys its item nodes on String(row.id) (screen.js:756), so a sink without an id column collapses every row onto one node — the kind of defect that renders as "one tag in the popular panel" and looks like a data problem. TagCount and FavoriteIndex are read as lists and therefore key on id: for TagCount the tag word is the identity (and /tag/{id} is the link), for FavoriteIndex it is the composite <user_id>:<article_id>. ArticleStats is only ever a nested singleton, so it keeps article_id as its pk and rides shell.yaml keys, which is derived automatically because a screen reads the entity (emit.cue:304,346). None of the three carries a seed: a per-subject sink's row appears with its first event, and the reading region carries data-empty-row='{"favorite_count":0}' so an article nobody has favorited renders 0 rather than nothing.
  13. No sink carries a foreign key to article. Deleting an article cascades its favorites and tag rows away; each cascaded DELETE is a CDC event whose back-filled before-image carries the dead article_id, and decision 11's key union is what makes the pipeline emit a final {article_id, 0} so the count does not freeze. If the sink column were a foreign key, that final POST would violate it, retry three times and wedge the stream — on every article deletion, which is a routine act. The cost of the alternative is an inert orphan row that nothing reads and no screen can reach. Wedging a pipeline is a class of failure the app cannot recover from; a stale row nobody looks at is not. FavoriteIndex.user_id keeps its FK, because no flow deletes a person and it is what lets the profile read filter by handle without denormalising one. What this entry bought is recorded here rather than discovered in profiling: with no foreign key from favorite_index.article_id to article there is no embedded article resource to join, so profile-favorites cannot state its list in one read and nests a live Article region per row instead — a server-computed GET per card, bounded by that list's own limit=20, re-fired on article and on app_user (decision 6). A wedged pipeline on every article deletion is still much the worse failure, so the trade stands — but a reader of this entry should be able to see that it has a price and where it is paid.
  14. Three composite uniques live in raw SQL, and their violations are the app's duplicate refusals. #Field.unique is per-field, so (user_id, article_id) on favorite, (follower_id, followed_id) on follow and (article_id, tag) on article_tag are 010_composite_uniques.sql. The refusal path matters and is stated once here: the Caddyfile injects Prefer: return=representation,resolution=ignore-duplicates on POSTs, and PostgREST's ignore-duplicates targets the primary key, which a client-minted uuid never collides on — so a repeated favorite reaches uq_favorite_pair and comes back 23505 as a readable .store-error ("You've already favorited this one."), not a silent merge. With the pill reading its true state from the probe that only happens on a double-press inside the optimistic window, and a refusal there is honest. This is what makes accept-favorite-once true at the database rather than at the button. Recorded alternative, not taken: ?on_conflict=user_id,article_id on the write would merge silently, but forms cannot express it; dropping the index would surrender the acceptance id outright.
  15. updated_at is a hidden {now} on the revise form, not a trigger. A column default fires only on INSERT, so "when it was last changed" needs a writer on UPDATE. resolveHidden (screen.js:222) resolves a hidden field's {now} to a submit-time ISO string, which is exactly one line of form contract. A third trigger next to the two this design already spends would be machinery for one column, and each trigger is raw SQL a reviewer must read. Accepted loss: the timestamp is the browser's clock at submit rather than the database's at commit, which can differ by the round-trip and by whatever the device's clock believes. Nothing in Conduit orders or filters by updated_at — every list is created_at.desc — so the drift is display-only, and the field is not user-facing on any frame.

A cover is a link the writer supplies, not an upload. capabilities.blobs is off, so there is no object store to put a file in and no resizer to put in front of it; a URL column costs a text field and reuses the treatment AppUser.image_url already has. The cost is the cost of any link: it can rot, and nothing here can notice. That is accepted for the same reason the profile picture accepts it.
  17. The editor asks for what it cannot refuse. Title and body are the only fields a publish is refused for; a standfirst, a cover and a tag are what separate a piece that gets read from one that scrolls past, and the reference apps steer writers toward all three rather than accepting their absence silently. So the bar states them as invitations with the reason attached, and each line reads met or unmet from its own control's :placeholder-shown — no state to keep, nothing to reset, and no second definition of "empty" to drift from the form's own. It is deliberately not a validation summary: nothing it lists blocks publishing, and a writer who wants none of it is not stopped.
  18. The strip under a piece lists that writer's earlier work, and the cursor is what excludes the piece being read. The local filter vocabulary has eq and the four cursor comparisons and no neq, so "everything else by this writer" cannot be expressed — created_at=lt.{created_at} can, and it drops the current piece by construction rather than by a rule someone has to maintain. It also reads better than it computes: earlier is a claim the ordering already supports. It reads the Article table the screen has synced anyway, so the strip costs a region and no new shape — which is the budget that decides what else this screen may carry.
  19. The reading cost is a stored generated column on the article, not a pipeline and not a number a writer types. It is a pure function of the body — words over 220 a minute, rounded up, floored at one — so the two alternatives both buy a way for the piece and its cost to disagree. A derived table would add a CDC round trip and a window in which a just-edited article advertises its old length; a typed field would let them disagree permanently, and would ask a writer for a number they are worse at estimating than arithmetic is. A generated column cannot drift, because there is no moment at which the body exists and the count does not. 220 words a minute is the median adult prose rate, and the figure Medium and Substack both round to — a reader who has calibrated against one of them has calibrated against this. The expression must be IMMUTABLE, which is what a generated column requires and what rules out anything that renders the markdown first: it splits on whitespace (regexp_split_to_array over btrim), so syntax counts as words. On heavily formatted prose that over-counts by a few per cent, which is accepted — the number is a reader's expectation-setter, not a measurement, and the alternative is a markdown parser in SQL.
  20. The writer's card sits after the body, and repeats the follow control there. The byline at the top sells the piece; the card at the foot sells the person, and that ask only makes sense to a reader who has just finished reading — which is the whole reason the card exists and the reason the ask belongs in it. Its identity costs no region: every value comes from the author embed the article row already carries, which is why bio joined that select rather than arriving as a second read — the byline and the card read the same row and so can never disagree about a name. Reversed from “no follow button here”: the objection was that two controls for one state are two places to be wrong about it, and that is only true of two controls with two sources of truth. These have one. The card's arms are the same two declared forms with the same filters, chosen by a probe with the same followed_id=eq.{author_id} — the same shape, so the two controls settle together on the same refresh and a reader cannot see “Follow” in one and “Following” in the other. What the top of the piece cannot do is catch the reader at the moment they have decided, because by then it is a screen and a half above them.
  21. Article bodies render as markdown through this app's own renderer module, and the terminal owns the half of that job which carries the safety property. The brief asks for a body "in markdown — headings, emphasis, links, lists, quotes and code, written plainly and read as formatting", and the terminal's built-in formats are plain and datetime alone: the honest options were a WASM hatch around a CommonMark parser, a Jessie handler (which cannot run — handlers fire only on drag), or shipping plain text and failing accept-body-markdown. The terminal's answer is the renderer role — data-text-format names a built-in format or an app module resolved by basename out of the route's files.renderers, exactly as data-handler resolves (screen.js:106-130) — so the format vocabulary grows per app rather than per platform release. This app declares shell/renderers/markdown.js on the article screen alone, the one screen that renders a body. A parser is not a platform affordance the way datetime is: there is no single right markdown subset, and an app that wants tables or maths should not be waiting on a terminal release to get them. The body binds as <div class="body" data-text="{body}" data-text-format="markdown"></div> and the screen styles the resulting elements at DESIGN.md's 65ch measure. The parser is app code; what it is allowed to produce is not. The parser returns a node description and the builder in render.js materialises it with createElement and textContent, never innerHTML — so there is no sanitizer in this design, and none is needed: a body containing <script> renders those literal characters, and a link whose scheme is not in the allowlist (http, https, mailto, or schemeless) renders as the author's own text rather than as a dead or dangerous link (render.js:23-38, accept-body-safe). A sanitizer is a denylist that must stay ahead of attackers; a builder that cannot express markup is a whitelist that cannot fall behind. The subset is deliberately smaller than CommonMark — paragraphs, ATX headings, unordered and ordered lists, blockquotes, fenced code, rules, hard breaks, and inline bold, italic, code, links and images; no tables, no nested lists, no reference links, no raw HTML — and the brief's Out of scope names the same four omissions, so the writer's vocabulary and the renderer's agree exactly rather than by luck. Rejected explicitly: a vendored markdown library at the terminal tier (capabilities.vendored exists but is outside the bijection, so nothing would check it), and a WASM hatch (a whole escape rung spent on a parser the platform should own — and it would have made decision 27 read "one", for the worse reason).
  22. Comment bodies are plain text; only article bodies are markdown. The same binding is one attribute away, so this is a choice and not a limit. The brief describes a comment as "its text, its writer, and when it was left" and describes an article body as markdown, in the same section — the asymmetry is the author's. A markdown reply is a formatting surface by another route, and the brief puts formatting surfaces (a toolbar, a live preview) out of scope; a one-line reply that renders # as a heading is a paper cut, not a feature. Comments still read as authored text under DESIGN.md's duality — serif, --primary, at the same measure — so the identity holds without the renderer. Reversible in one attribute if the ux seat argues otherwise.
  23. The two reading modes are two routes, not two lists on one screen. Only top-level regions drive the state machine and there is no arbitration between siblings: each sets empty or populated from its own rows (screen.js:843-845), so the last to refresh wins. On a combined home the loser would be the feed's empty state — precisely the one the brief singles out ("tells a reader with no follows yet how to fill it, rather than showing them nothing") and precisely the one accept-feed-follows-only checks. As routes they also earn nav chrome, a back stack entry and a shareable address for free, and the nav row the terminal builds reads Community · Your Feed · New Article · Settings — the home route glossed Community so the word Conduit belongs to the masthead alone (decision 26) — which is the whole navigation this app needs. Rejected: a Zag tabs widget (widgets feed form values, not region visibility — widget.js, screen.js:696-712), and CSS :target (the hash belongs to the router). The general rule this compile follows: a screen with more than one top-level region styles empty and populated alike and lets each region's own data-empty sentence speak; a screen with one styles empty as the whole screen.

Any region that must contain another region is a one-row list region, never a singleton. hydrateRegion's singleton branch binds attributes, texts and hatches and never calls syncNested; only the list branch does (screen.js:726,785 vs the singleton branch at 847-880), and the top-level scan skips any region whose ancestor is a region. A [data-live] nested under a singleton is therefore hydrated by nobody — dead markup that lints clean and renders nothing, which is the worst failure shape available. So /article/:slug, /settings's me root, /editor's me root, /editor/:id, both profile headers and each favorited row's article preview are all lists that happen to match one row: they bind identically, they recurse for their children, and their forms get a real row id. Nothing is lost — the two states differ only in name here — and the alternative, a singleton root, would cost that screen every nested region it has: tags, comments, stats, and three probes.

Long lists page by a keyset cursor the row already carries; four lists cap at twenty and say so, for cost rather than for a limit. limit and offset ride the filter string and work (measured against the running cluster), but there is no arithmetic anywhere in the binding grammar, so a screen cannot compute offset + 20. The way out is to page on a value that already exists: the twentieth card's own created_at, interpolated into an ordinary link — <a data-route="older" data-param-when="{created_at}">Older writing →</a>, revealed by .list > *:nth-child(20) .older { display: block } — and /older/:when filters created_at=lt.{param.when}&limit=20 and carries the same link again. There is no last page, no page count, and no number a screen had to invent. Two alternatives were live and both were rejected. A literal page-number ladder (/page/1…/page/5) is a fixed ceiling with dead links the day the sixth page exists. And — the interesting one — a pipeline-materialised page index: a pipeline can do arithmetic where a screen cannot, so a sink table of one row per page carrying its literal offset is genuinely expressible, and a pipeline is where the arithmetic a screen cannot do is allowed to happen. It was rejected here for cost and honesty: a whole entity and a fourth pipeline bought for one link, recomputed on every article write, and lagging the list it indexes by CDC latency — so the page ladder would disagree with the list it sits under for seconds after every publication. Keyset paging is exact, instant, and free. Three details of the reveal and the order are decisions rather than styling. The rule is :nth-child(20) and not :last-child: a three-article community would otherwise end its list with “Older writing →” one tap after being told to write the first piece, and land on “You've reached the beginning.” — a dead end offered as a way forward. Every paged list orders created_at.desc,id.desc, so the sequence is deterministic across the boundary; the residual loss is an accepted one and belongs here rather than deferred inside a test: two rows sharing a created_at to the microsecond straddle a strict lt. cursor and the second is dropped, because one :param cannot carry a composite cursor and there is no arithmetic to build one. It is reachable — two pieces inserted in one transaction, or seed data — and it is the price of paging without a page number. And feed pages to feed-older, never to older: the cheap version of this design pointed both at the community list, which silently dropped the follow filter under a heading the reader had no reason to distrust.

The consequence is worth stating rather than assuming: DESIGN.md's “don't animate longer than 300ms” is therefore a property of the terminal, verified once against its defaults, and not a rule this app restates in its own stylesheets — where it would have nothing to govern, since a screen that animates has taken over a transition the terminal drives.
  30. The two pipelines still written as bloblang declare the browser tier unsupported rather than quietly broken. A cdc pipeline that is not a fold must ship a browser shim (the file must exist), and that shim's contract is (rows) => singleRowObject upserted on id — but favorite-index and tag-recount both emit a keyed array of sink rows, one per subject, which that contract cannot express. Each is therefore a stub that throws naming this decision, rather than a plausible-looking function: an unsupported tier that fails loudly is a known gap; one that returns a number is a lie.

    favorite-recount is no longer among them: it declares a browser fold (decision 37), which states a keyed aggregate the shim contract cannot. The remaining two are the same conversion, unstarted.

  31. The sink carries a read watermark, and the browser resumes the count over the rows that watermark excludes. A sink lags its source by the whole CDC loop — near a second — so favouriting a piece changed its heart instantly while the number beside it sat still.

    The browser transform is declared as a fold: empty(key), step(acc, row), combine(a, b), result(acc). Two laws no type states. combine is associative with empty as its identity, so a fold splits and total = fold(counted) ⊕ fold(not yet counted). And the accumulator IS the sink row, so the sink reads back as a partial fold — which is why an average would store sum and n rather than the quotient. The fold also replaces the browser shim, whose (rows) => singleRow contract could not state a keyed aggregate at all.

    The watermark is a separate column and must be. article_stats.counted_txid is the newest favorite.txid the count includes, emitted by the transform from the rows it actually read. The sink's own txid cannot serve: it is stamped when the sink row is written, strictly after the read it summarises, so every favourite committed in that window looks older than the sink while never having been counted. Reading it that way silently drops the reader's own favourite and settles on a count one too low beside a filled heart. Clicking slowly never opens the window; a reader clicking quickly lives inside it.

    Contributions collapse on the natural key. Toggling fast leaves several pending rows for one (user_id, article_id) pair in the optimistic overlay, and the unique constraint means the server can hold exactly one — summing them showed totals no state of the database could produce. A row the sink has already counted also outranks a pending duplicate of itself, which is what stops the two being added together when the sink's update overtakes the row's own confirmation.

    Both sides of the comparison read the local collections and never the server. An earlier attempt put the same arithmetic in the markup, where relational operators are not maintainable, so the gate asked PostgREST while the value came from Electric and the count dipped between two readers of one table.

    The container keeps its bloblang. rpk embeds goja and can run this module — a revision of this work did exactly that, on the argument that one implementation needs no equivalence check — but redpanda-connect lint rejects a broken mapping and accepts any JavaScript string whatsoever, so the sharing cost more in build-time validation than it bought. For a plain count the two sides are three one-line functions apart. Convert a container transform only where the aggregate is substantial enough that two expressions of it could diverge.

    The fold declares an inverse, and a retraction is a row rather than an absence. unstep(acc, row) undoes step, which makes this fold a group rather than only a monoid. Not every aggregate has one — count and sum do, max and min do not, since removing the extreme leaves no way to recover the next from the accumulator alone — so it is optional, and a fold without it waits for the loop.

    Un-favouriting SETS Favorite.deleted_at instead of deleting the row, and the pair stays unique, so there is exactly one row per reader per piece forever: the table cannot grow by toggling and needs no reaper. The point is not storage, it is that a retraction the reader can still SEE needs no memory of what was removed. An earlier revision kept removed rows in a per-tab map with a rule for when to retire them; that map, its retirement rule and its retraction bound are all gone, and the retraction now survives a reload and is visible to the reader's other tabs.

    The count reads only active rows; the WATERMARK reads all of them, retracted included. Filtering the transform's read to active rows would leave a just-retracted row above the watermark, where the browser reads it as a change the sink has not seen and subtracts it a second time.

    The two directions are one rule. A row places itself relative to the sink by txid — the last version the server acknowledged — and whether anything of the reader's sits on top of it here. Zero unseen changes means the sink is current; one means the row has moved once, and is worth step or unstep according to where it now points; two means it has moved and moved back, and is worth nothing, because assertion and retraction alternate. That parity is what stops re-favouriting inside one loop counting twice on a sink still holding the first assertion.

    Three or more unseen changes cannot be told from one, because the row keeps only its latest txid. That is the bound this design accepts in exchange for holding no per-tab memory, and it costs at most one for at most one loop.

  32. Press, over Conduit-classic and Terminal. The designer charter requires at least three directions with recorded verdicts before an identity commits, and DESIGN.md carries the comparison. Press — warm paper ground, near-black ink, one deep pine accent spent only where something is created or committed, and a serif/sans duality in which everything a person wrote is serif and everything the app says about it is sans — was chosen because the duality does the work a second colour would otherwise have to do, which lets the palette stay quiet across a long scroll of previews; because the article body's reading measure is native to the identity rather than a special case on its most important screen; and because the dark twin keeps its warmth by lowering value rather than shifting hue, so it is a token swap and never a redesign. Conduit-classic — the RealWorld reference look, white ground, bright #5CB85C, all-sans — was rejected as the identity this exercise exists to beat: the green competes with every preview title, and an all-sans setting makes a body read like a form field. Terminal — near-black in both appearances, monospace metadata — was rejected for flattering short chrome and punishing long prose, and because a dark-only ground makes the light appearance a retrofit when the brief asks for both to read correctly (accept-dual-appearance). The contract carried forward: the token names are the schema contract (colors, dark, rounded, spacing travel verbatim into the program), so the values may evolve without a migration — and a screen stylesheet may consume them with var() but may never redeclare one, which check-screens enforces as an error.
    Three bindings this entry has to make explicit, because DESIGN.md legislates them and an omission is an answer. The chrome. #Design.shell (schema.cue:31-35) defaults to {bg: "neutral", fg: "primary", rule: "border"}, which emit.cue:526-528 resolves to --shell-fg: var(--primary) and shell.css paints onto body with nav a { color: inherit } — so the default would render the nav row and “velvet-crow-4 · sign out” in ink. This app declares shell: {bg: "neutral", fg: "secondary", rule: "border"}: the nav is chrome and reads at --secondary, which is what every frame draws, and the consequence is named rather than discovered — this also becomes body's default colour, which every screen overrides for authored text anyway, since authored text is exactly what the type roles already promote to --primary. The wordmark. DESIGN.md's one absolute Don't is “chrome in serif”, and home's masthead sets Conduit in serif at display size. That is the single exemption this identity grants, and it is granted here rather than left to be argued: a masthead is a publication's name-plate, not navigation — it is the one place the app speaks in the voice of the writing rather than about it, it appears on one route, and it is never a link to anywhere. The navigation gloss for / is therefore Community in sans, so the word Conduit appears once per screen and always as the name-plate. Type. The five DESIGN.md roles are bound to surfaces in the Screens intro's role table, and test-dual-appearance carries the matching assertion beside its no-literal-hex clause: a screen stylesheet may set family and size only through the role classes, never inline. Colour had a checkable rule and type had none, which left the duality that is this identity resting on prose and on frames measured by eye.
  33. Escape hatches: none. Argued, not assumed. The ranked ladder is pre-compiled WASM behind a CUE contract, then an external API endpoint, then a container with a declared browser-tier shim; this app takes no rung of it. Everything Conduit does is schema, access policy, trigger, pipeline or screen markup: ten entities and their CEL constraints, five raw migrations, three cdc pipelines, twelve screens, and zero handlers — the only event source wired to a handler is drag and nothing here reorders. The one Jessie module this app ships is the article screen's markdown renderer, and a renderer is a declared seam rather than a hatch: it runs in a compartment with nothing endowed, and what it may produce is the terminal's tag and attribute allowlist rather than the module's own choice. The five raw migrations are the one thing that looks like an escape and is not: rawMigrations is a declared seam in schema.cue, its files are checked-in SQL a reviewer reads, they are emitted in the same numbered sequence as the derived DDL, and the two triggers among them exist because the platform's own alternative would be wrong rather than merely absent (decisions 3 and 10 each say why). The one place an escape was genuinely on the table was markdown rendering, and the answer was the terminal's renderer role (decision 16) — which is the doctrine working: the platform owns what a rendering may safely become, and the app owns only which markdown subset it wants. Consequently the emitted bayt.cue redeclares out.cluster unchanged and the program's cluster: unification carries nothing — the two places a hatch would be visible are both empty, which is what "none" has to mean to be checkable.
  34. The reader's own identity in the strip is a link, not a label. capabilities.auth.self hands the terminal the route that is a person's own page (/profile/:handle, its param filled from the session user) and the column the name they chose lives in (app_user.display_name), and the strip draws name · handle · sign out with the name linking there (shell.js:198-241). Three things were decided together and are one decision because each is only right given the others. The name is read live, not stamped from the token: the JWT carries handle and the auth service is app-agnostic by doctrine — it inserts (id, handle) and knows nothing of a display name — so a token-stamped strip would keep reading the old name until the next sign-in, on the one surface a person sees on every screen, while every byline had already changed. The strip follows the same name-then-handle rule as a byline, .name:empty collapsing for anyone who has set none (decision 23's rule, applied to chrome), because a person named one way in the content and another way in the chrome is the inconsistency the rule exists to prevent. And settings comes off the strip (strip: false, the one opt-out the nav has): one's own page now leads there, a second top-level path to a screen visited once crowded the one identity that is on screen always, and the strip did not fit at a phone width with both. What hangs on it is not cosmetic: a person's own writing lives on profile, and the only other door to one's own is the “last published” list on editor — so without this link the one piece of identity a reader has on every screen is the one that leads nowhere.
  35. A save is a favorite with its derived half deleted, and that deletion is the feature. Bookmark is owned on user_id exactly as Favorite is, but where a favorite has two public faces — ArticleStats for the count everyone sees, FavoriteIndex for the list somebody else can read — a save has none. Nobody may see that you saved a piece, no number moves when you do, and so there is no sink, no pipeline, and none of the seconds of lag the derived faces cost. The reading list is therefore read straight off the owned table, which is what lets it be ordered by save time, the thing a reader means by “my list”; profile-favorites cannot do that, because its order has to come from a sink.

    Two alternatives were rejected. Folding saves into Favorite: one gesture cannot mean both “this was good” and “not yet”, and a reader who hearts a piece to find it again has corrupted the only public signal the app has. A public save count: it is the obvious next feature and it would change what people save — a reading list is a private intention, and an intention with an audience becomes a performance.

    ACCEPTED COST, and the measurement changed what it says. The star's probe is the seventh Electric shape on a list screen, and home already opened six (article, app_user, article_tag, article_stats, favorite, tag_count — counted live, one shape per table however many cards draw it). Six is Chrome's per-origin connection ceiling on HTTP/1.1, so the pool is fully consumed by long-polls and a write waits for one of them to return. This was written down as a mild cost and then measured, and it is not mild — from PerformanceResourceTiming on the running cluster:

    screenlive shapeswritestalledserver
    settings2PATCH app_user, 183 ms1 ms181 ms
    tag7POST bookmark, 12 151 ms12 073 ms78 ms

    Seventy-eight milliseconds of server against twelve seconds of stalled: the write is not slow, it is queued behind six long-polls for a connection. Two things follow, and only one of them is about this feature. The first: this is not a regression the save arm introduced — at six shapes the pool was already exhausted, so favoriting from home stalled exactly as long before the seventh shape existed (measured at 7 855 ms on the same page), and article has been over the ceiling at eight since it was written. The optimistic arms are what have hidden it: the star and the pill fill on the press, so the reader sees the write land and the twelve seconds are spent invisibly. The second, which is the one that matters: this is now the app's dominant defect, it is transport and not schema, and it has a known answer — the cluster's own :8443 listener is TLS and therefore h2, which has no per-origin cap, while the human door on :8080 is plain HTTP/1.1 because a browser will not negotiate h2 in clear. Gating a feature every reading surface has on that artifact would be optimising the wrong thing; leaving the number unwritten would be worse. The rule it leaves for the next feature: on this app a new region is free and a new table is not.
  36. A cover carries a credit line, and it is one nullable column rather than a caption system. The picture on a piece is almost always somebody else's, and showing it without saying whose is a claim the app has not earned — every surface this one is measured against runs a credit under the image. What it is not is a caption: no rich text, no link, no second alt story, 120 characters. That ceiling is the decision. A caption field invites a second body — writers put sentences in it, then markdown, then links — and the piece already has a body and a standfirst for that.

    It costs no region. The caption lives inside the cover's own one-row region, which exists already because a bare <img> bound to an unset column would hold its box open on every piece without a picture; so a credit renders exactly where a cover does and nowhere else, and :empty collapses it where the writer supplied none — the same rule that collapses an unset display name in a byline, against the same problem. Nullable rather than required for the same reason: a writer who took the photograph has nobody to credit, and a required field would put “own work” on half the pieces in the app.
  37. A recount emits the group its event touched, and no others. The count stays absolute — it is the length of the fetched set, never a delta, which is what decision 9 asks for — but the scope of the emission is one article. Re-emitting every group was the earlier shape, and it was expensive in a way that reached the screen: merge-duplicates is ON CONFLICT DO UPDATE, the restamp_txid trigger fires on any UPDATE including one writing an unchanged count, so a favorite anywhere restamped every article_stats row. This mapping already refuses that for foreign messages, for exactly this reason; it simply had not applied the rule within its own table's events.

    Two things follow. The write amplification goes: one row moves per event, not the whole sink. And article_stats.txid starts meaning “this count was last recomputed”, where before it meant “something, somewhere, was recounted” — which is the difference between a gate an optimistic counter can trust and one that flickers (decision 38). Measured: a favorite on one piece now moves that row's txid and leaves the other five untouched.

    ACCEPTED LOSS. Recounting every article made a dropped event self-healing — the next event for any article repaired all of them. Narrowed, a dropped event leaves that one article's count stale until something touches it again. The absolute read survives; the incidental repair does not, and no test covered it, which is its own argument for writing it down here.
  38. The favorite count IS projected optimistically, and what made that hard was that the reader is inside the aggregate and cannot see the rest of it. The arm was always optimistic — the row lands locally under 100 ms — while the number beside it is ArticleStats, everyone's, a CDC loop behind. Favorite is private, so a browser holds its own rows and no one else's; the count holds everybody. The reader is a term in a sum whose other terms they may not read.

    What the terminal shows is others + intent. Intent is local fact and applies at once, in either direction. others needs the reader's own place in the public total, and that is a property of the READ that produced the total rather than of the data now — so it is stated by whoever performed the read, not reconstructed by whoever consumes it. favorite-count emits it per reader into FavoriteCount, which RLS keeps private because a count discloses nobody.

    An earlier attempt put the same arithmetic in the markup and failed on a read skew — a relational comparison is not maintainable, so the gate asked PostgREST while the value came from the Electric-synced collection, and the number dipped between them (measured 7, 8, 7, 8, the dip at 178 ms). Four later attempts tried to infer the reader's contribution from their own row and each failed at a different number of changes, because a bounded row cannot carry unbounded history. The full account, with what each attempt cost and the platform facts it rests on, is plugins/pronto/docs/2026-08-12-derived-counts-the-reader-is-inside.md.

    Two findings survive from that search regardless: a singleton region wrapping a templated child adopts that child's template and clears everything else in it, and decision 37, which was discovered while chasing this and is worth keeping on its own.

  39. How far into a piece the reader is, is drawn by the scroller — not by code. A progress hairline is the one reading affordance every long-form surface has settled on, and the obvious way to build it is a scroll listener, which this app cannot have: handlers fire on drag alone and decision 25 keeps the compartment count at zero. The affordance is available anyway, because CSS grew the capability: animation-timeline: scroll(root block) binds a two-frame keyframe to the document's own scroll progress, so the bar is a stylesheet rule with nothing to schedule and nothing to run on a frame that does not scroll. Two details are load-bearing rather than tidy. It is wrapped in @supports (animation-timeline: scroll()), because a browser without scroll-driven animations would run the same declaration as an ordinary animation of zero duration with both fill and paint the bar full on arrival — the one wrong answer, and worse than absence; unsupported, the rule outside the block leaves it display: none. The same wrong answer arrives by a second road and is closed the same way: a piece too short to scroll leaves the timeline inactive, which applies no effect at all, so the rule declares transform: scaleX(0) outright and lets the animation override it while it runs — measured, not assumed (a gone frame painted a full accent line before it was). And the storybook hides it: a frame is a box on a page of boxes and a viewport-fixed element would draw one accent line across the top of the review page per frame, none of them about the frame beneath it. The accent is spent here — the only accent in this screen's chrome — because progress through a piece is the reading surface talking about itself, not navigation, which DESIGN.md holds to secondary.
